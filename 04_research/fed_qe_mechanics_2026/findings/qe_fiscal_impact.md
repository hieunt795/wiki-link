## Finding

QE performs a "maturity transformation" on the consolidated public sector balance sheet (Fed + Treasury), effectively converting long-term fixed-rate government debt into overnight floating-rate debt. This significantly increases the sovereign's exposure to rising interest rates.

### The Swap Mechanism
- The Treasury issues long-term bonds (e.g., 30-year fixed).
- The Fed buys these bonds and pays by issuing reserves (interest-paying overnight liabilities).
- From the perspective of the **taxpayer (consolidated public sector)**, the long-term debt has been "canceled out" and replaced by a floating-rate mortgage [RAW-CLIP A new Fed-Treasury Accord].

### Fiscal Impact
- When interest rates rise, the Fed must pay more interest (IORB) on its reserve liabilities.
- Since the Fed's income from the long-term bonds is fixed (the coupons), its net interest margin shrinks or turns negative.
- This results in deferred assets (losses) for the Fed and a cessation of remittances to the Treasury, effectively increasing the net interest cost of the federal debt [RAW-CLIP A new Fed-Treasury Accord].
- In Japan, this same mechanism turned the public sector into a "giant carry trade" paying floating and receiving fixed [RAW-CLIP Japan I].

## Evidence Chain
1. Treasury issues fixed-rate debt → 2. Fed buys debt via QE → 3. Fed issues floating-rate reserves → 4. Consolidated sector interest cost becomes sensitive to short-term rate hikes [LLM].

## Limitations / Caveats
This analysis ignores the potential stimulus effect of QE which might increase tax revenues by boosting GDP, potentially offsetting the higher interest costs [LLM].
