## Point Of Conflict
[RAW-CLIP] The source describes the Warsh line as the idea that shrinking the Fed's balance sheet "frees up" room for lower interest rates.

[RAW-CLIP] The same source rejects that inference when the balance sheet was only meeting structural demand for reserves.

[LLM] The contradiction is not over whether the Fed can shrink its balance sheet at all. It is over what that shrinkage means economically.

## Warsh Side
[LLM] On the Warsh side, balance-sheet reduction is treated as the removal of stimulus and as a precondition for returning rate cuts to the center of policy.

[LLM] That view assumes the central bank footprint itself is a form of active easing that can be reversed and reallocated.

## Critique
[LLM] The critique says that if reserves were being supplied for plumbing reasons, the Fed was not delivering extra stimulus in the first place.

[LLM] Under that interpretation, shrinking the balance sheet after structural demand has fallen does not create a new stock of stimulus that can be redeployed.

[LLM] Instead, it just reflects a change in the quantity of reserves needed for financial stability and payment-system functioning.

## Why The Distinction Matters
[LLM] The distinction matters because it changes the policy mapping between size and rates.

[LLM] If balance-sheet size is mostly neutral plumbing, then rate cuts should be driven by macro conditions, not by some presumed reserve "room" created by QT.

[LLM] If balance-sheet size is itself stimulus, then QT can be marketed as creating room for lower rates.

[LLM] The source argues for the first interpretation and treats the second as a conceptual error.
