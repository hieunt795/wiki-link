## Core Doctrine
[RAW-CLIP] Warsh is presented as wanting the Fed to buy less government debt, shrink its footprint on markets, and do less overall so that it can preserve and strengthen independence.

[RAW-CLIP] The same source says that he would treat the balance sheet as a tool for reducing distortion, not as a permanent source of stimulus.

[RAW-CLIP] In the Accord framing, the Treasury should own the maturity structure of government debt, while the Fed should limit itself to short-term bonds or collateralized lending if it needs to supply reserves.

[LLM] Taken together, that is a doctrine of separation: the Treasury manages fiscal duration risk, while the Fed manages only the short-rate operating framework and the minimum liquidity needed for market functioning.

## Operational Rules
[LLM] The first rule is footprint minimization: keep the Fed from becoming the default buyer of long-duration risk.

[LLM] The second rule is credit-allocation restraint: if the state wants to subsidize housing, mortgages, or other sectors, that choice should sit with the politically accountable fiscal authority.

[LLM] The third rule is maturity sovereignty: the Treasury should decide the government's duration profile, and the Fed should not undo that decision by swapping long debt into overnight reserves.

[LLM] The fourth rule is crisis discipline: extraordinary monetization should require explicit political authorization, not automatic central-bank balance-sheet expansion.

## Balance Sheet Meaning
[RAW-CLIP] The clipping argues that shrinking the balance sheet is supposed to free the Fed from a bloated footprint and create more policy space.

[LLM] In this doctrine, a smaller balance sheet is not valuable because it mechanically forces lower rates.

[LLM] It is valuable because it narrows the Fed's role to liquidity management and short-run rate control, while pushing duration choice and credit allocation back to the Treasury or Congress.

## Tension Points
[LLM] The main tension is that the doctrine assumes the Fed can shrink without breaking the reserve floor.

[LLM] If structural reserve demand is large, the Fed still has to provide enough liabilities for payment-system stability even if it no longer wants to hold a large asset portfolio.

[LLM] That means the doctrine is operationally feasible only if Treasury issuance, reserve demand, and the Fed's chosen tools are aligned well enough to keep the floor system functioning.

[LLM] The political attraction of the doctrine is that it makes the Fed look less like a quasi-fiscal allocator and more like a narrow monetary operator.
