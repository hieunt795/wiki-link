## Five Purposes of Reserve Requirements (Bindseil 2004a)

| Purpose | Mechanism | Remuneration |
|---------|-----------|--------------|
| **Monetary policy** | Unremunerated or below-market reserves create a deadweight tax on lending; varying requirements ≡ varying interest rates | Unremunerated or below-market |
| **Liquidity management** | Averaging provisions let banks smooth daily cash flows → reduces money market volatility | Can be remunerated at market rate (then no monetary role) |
| **Structural liquidity** | Creating or enhancing a desired liquidity shortage via the liabilities side of the CB balance sheet | Either |
| **Revenue** | Unremunerated reserves invested by CB earn a return funding policy functions (e.g. UK Cash Ratio Deposits) | Unremunerated |
| **Sectoral behaviour** | Differentiated requirements to incentivise domestic vs foreign lending or specific sector exposure | Varies |

## Reserve Requirement Resurgence as Alternative to Rate Hikes

In economies facing inflationary pressure from capital inflows: raising rates attracts more capital inflows (worsens the problem); tightening reserve requirements achieves domestic tightening without increasing the rate differential that drives inflows. [RAW-CLIP]

## Voluntary vs Excess Free Reserves

**Voluntary free reserves**: Banks self-insure against overdraft risk by holding small voluntary buffers. These are stable and time-invariant even as the policy rate changes (Disyatat 2008). [RAW-CLIP]

**Excess free reserves**: Banks hold reserves they do not want and seek to trade away. Presence at system level is a result of CB actions, not bank preferences. A critical mass of excess reserves pushes interbank rates toward the floor (deposit rate or zero), potentially creating **looser-than-intended monetary conditions**. [RAW-CLIP]

## Asset-Driven vs Liability-Driven Balance Sheets

**Liability-driven (shortage of liquidity)**:
- Balance sheet grows as nominal GDP rises → more transactions → more reserve demand
- CB meets demand through lending operations (assets side)
- CB can dictate terms of operations (collateral, rate), earns seigniorage income
- Typical profile: inflation-targeting CB with floating exchange rate, no debt monetisation [RAW-CLIP]

**Asset-driven (surplus of liquidity)**:
- Balance sheet grows from CB policy decisions on the asset side (FX intervention, government debt monetisation)
- Liabilities characterised by excess free reserves or absorption operations
- CB in a weaker position to dictate terms; operations can cost money rather than earn it
- Policy transmission weakened — banks trade excess reserves among themselves, pushing rates toward the floor [RAW-CLIP]

## Two Main Causes of Surplus Liquidity

1. **FX intervention**: CB buys foreign assets to resist appreciation → reserve creation outstrips natural demand for domestic liabilities. Often requires costly sterilisation (issuance of CB securities or term repos). This is common in South East Asia. [RAW-CLIP]
2. **Government debt monetisation**: Unsustainable fiscal policies force CB to credit bank accounts in lieu of government funding → historically associated with high inflation when combined with productive capacity destruction. [RAW-CLIP]

## Impact of Surplus on Policy Transmission

- CB loses ability to control the first step of transmission (short-term market rate floors at deposit rate)
- CB may be in a weaker position to dictate collateral terms
- Operations shift from income-generating (lending) to income-costing (absorption)
- Many central banks in South East Asia have faced negative capital from sterilisation losses + revaluation losses on foreign reserves [RAW-CLIP]
