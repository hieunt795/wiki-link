## Regime Break
[RAW-BOOK Ch.17] Watts and Wray describe US late-1960s and early-1970s inflation as the combination of Vietnam-era demand pressure and sharp oil-price increases after OPEC's formation.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.17] The same source states that OPEC oil prices quadrupled in 1973 and created large cost shocks for oil-dependent economies such as the United States and Japan.

[RAW-BOOK §Inflation] The IMF text says a rise in the price of key goods such as energy can trigger chained price and wage increases across the economy if monetary policy is not sufficiently firm.

[LLM] In the US case, stagflation was therefore not a pure demand boom or a pure supply shock; it was a regime interaction between demand pressure, imported energy costs, and nominal accommodation.

## Why the Phillips Curve Failed
[RAW-BOOK Ch.18] Watts and Wray split US inflation-unemployment observations into 1948-1969, 1970-1980, and 1981-2015, and describe the 1970-1980 observations as inconsistent with a stable Phillips curve.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.18] They say the 1970-1980 data seemed to imply a positively sloping relationship, which was treated as a collapse of the simple Keynesian Phillips-curve tradeoff.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.18] They also link Lerner's stagflation idea to the empirical instability that began in the late 1960s.

[LLM] This is the analytic pivot: policymakers could no longer assume that a bit more inflation reliably bought lower unemployment.

## Structural Pressures
[RAW-BOOK Ch.17] Watts and Wray describe the mid-1970s crisis as involving rising inflation, profit squeeze, declining productivity growth, and increasing external competition.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.17] They state that lower investment, weaker aggregate demand growth, and harsh monetary and fiscal contractions combined to create stagflation in the second half of the 1970s.

[LLM] That makes US stagflation a multi-cause regime rather than a single oil-shock episode.

## Policy Consequence
[RAW-BOOK Ch.2.2.2] Warjiyo and Juhro say the collapse of Bretton Woods was followed by inflationary pressures and depression in several countries in early 1974.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.2.2.2] They say central banks initially focused on unemployment after the 1973 oil shock, but inflation expectations accelerated and pushed central banks toward monetary targeting.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.2.2.2] They identify 1979 and the second oil shock as the point when many central banks began to focus on inflation control rather than leaning against unemployment bumps.

[LLM] Volcker's Fed sits inside this broader regime shift from unemployment stabilization toward credibility-restoring inflation control.
