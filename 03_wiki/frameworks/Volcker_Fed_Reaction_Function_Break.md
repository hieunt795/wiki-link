## Reaction Function Break
[RAW-BOOK Ch.7] Warjiyo and Juhro say Fed policy implementation differed significantly before and after 1979.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.7] They report that the inflation target was higher in 1960-1979 than in 1979-1996.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.7] They also report that the Fed's response coefficient to inflation was larger in the second period, indicating a stronger reaction to inflationary shocks.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.7] With beta below 1, a central bank raises the nominal interest rate less than inflation, lowering the real interest rate and allowing destabilization to continue.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.7] In the second period, nominal rate hikes were larger than the inflation increase, so the real interest rate tended to rise.

[LLM] This is the technical core of the Volcker shock: the Fed stopped validating inflation through negative or insufficiently positive real rates.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.7, Table 7.3] The table reports the pre-Volcker, Volcker-Greenspan, and post-1982 reaction-function estimates.

[LLM] The post-1982 configuration is especially inflation-first because the inflation coefficient is far above one while the output-gap coefficient is negative and statistically weak in the source's description.

## Monetary Targeting Window
[RAW-BOOK Ch.7] Warjiyo and Juhro say that from 1979 to 1982 the Fed operationally practiced monetary targeting, using the monetary base, which ultimately produced larger interest-rate shocks.

[RAW-BOOK Ch.2.2.2] They also say that after the second oil shock in 1979, many central banks shifted toward inflation control rather than leaning against unemployment.

[LLM] Volcker's monetary targeting was therefore both an operating procedure and a credibility device: it signaled that inflation control would dominate short-run output smoothing.

## Policy Meaning
[RAW-BOOK Ch.7] The source states that since Paul Volcker's leadership, US monetary policy focused on more organized inflation control.

[LLM] The durable lesson is not that monetary aggregates are always the right target.

[LLM] The durable lesson is that a central bank facing stagflation may need a reaction function that raises real rates when inflation rises, even when output costs are severe.
