## Overview
Referencing the original 1951 Accord that established Fed independence, the **2026 Proposal** (associated with Kevin Warsh and Treasury Secretary Bessent) seeks to decouple the Fed from fiscal operations. Contrary to fears of "fiscal dominance," the proposal aims to strengthen Fed independence by making it "do less" in political areas [RAW-CLIP].

## Three Pillars of the New Accord

### 1. Debt Maturity Sovereignty
The Treasury assumes full responsibility for the maturity structure of US government debt [RAW-CLIP].
- **Problem:** QE effectively undid the Treasury's efforts to lock in long-term rates by swapping long-term debt for overnight reserves [RAW-CLIP].
- **Solution:** The Fed should only hold short-term bonds or lend via collateral (ECB-style), leaving interest rate risk management to the Treasury. If the Fed must buy long-term debt for "market function," it must swap the risk back to the Treasury [RAW-CLIP].

### 2. Exit from Credit Allocation
The Fed ceases "taking from Peter to pay Paul" through targeted credit support (e.g., Mortgage-Backed Securities) [RAW-CLIP].
- **Logic:** Subsidizing specific sectors (housing vs. business) is a political decision. If such support is needed, the Treasury should conduct it directly and be held politically accountable [RAW-CLIP].
- **Implementation:** Rapid runoff or transfer of MBS holdings to the Treasury's balance sheet.

### 3. "The Power to Say NO" in Crisis
The Accord seeks to prevent the automatic monetization of deficits during crises (as seen in 2020) [RAW-CLIP].
- **Mechanism:** A stronger anti-inflation mandate. If a crisis requires extraordinary measures, Congress must explicitly authorize them (e.g., a TARP-like structure), rather than the Fed acting as an "independent" source of $3T in new money [RAW-CLIP].

## Implications for 2026
- **Smaller Balance Sheet:** A central tenet is shrinking the Fed's footprint to reduce "price distortions" and restore "policy space" [RAW-CLIP].
- **Conflict with Ample Reserves:** This framework directly challenges the "ample reserves" regime if the Treasury's debt issuance patterns pull liquidity faster than the Fed can provide it via short-term operations [LLM].
- **Yield Curve Control Risk:** If the Fed stops buying long-term debt, the Treasury must find private buyers. This could lead to higher long-term yields even if the Fed cuts short-term rates (the "Warsh Swap") [LLM].

## Systemic Risks
- **Transition Volatility:** Shifting the maturity management from Fed to Treasury could cause significant repricing in the long end of the yield curve.
- **Fiscal Dominance by Proxy:** While intended to increase independence, the "Accord" relies on the Treasury being "responsible" with its new debt-maturity powers, which may not hold during fiscal stress [LLM].
