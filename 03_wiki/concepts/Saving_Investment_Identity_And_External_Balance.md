## Overview

The **saving-investment identity** is the second fundamental accounting relationship from SNA national accounts used in IMF macroeconomic analysis:

  CAB = S − I

where S is economy-wide gross saving and I is gross investment. In a closed economy, S = I by definition. In an open economy, any excess of investment over saving must be financed by foreign saving — the economy runs a current account deficit. [RAW-BOOK Ch.2]

This is the mirror image of the income-absorption identity (CAB = GNDI − A). Both are *ex-post* accounting relationships that always hold by definition.

## How It Works

Three immediate analytical implications:

**Excess investment (I > S):** The economy borrows the difference from abroad, running a CAB deficit. Foreign saving fills the gap. [RAW-BOOK Ch.2]

**Policy levers:** A CAB deficit can be reduced by (a) raising domestic saving — household, corporate, or government — or (b) reducing investment. The identity is silent on which lever is more feasible or desirable. [RAW-BOOK Ch.2]

**Sectoral decomposition:** S and I split by sector (private, government). A fiscal deficit (G > T) not offset by higher private saving mechanically increases the CAB deficit — this is the origin of the "twin deficits" analytical link. [LLM — the twin deficits link is behavioral and not a pure identity; the identity only establishes the accounting constraint.]

The source is explicit: this identity is ex-post and "does not provide an explanation of any imbalances in the economy, the underlying behavior of economic agents, or the desirability of a particular imbalance." [RAW-BOOK Ch.2, p.19]

## Evidence and Sources

[RAW-BOOK Ch.2, pp.17-19] Ouanes and Thakur derive CAB = S − I from the expenditure and income identities: GDP = C + I + G + NX and GDP = C + S + T, yielding S − T − I − G = NX, i.e. (S − I) − (G − T) = CAB. They describe this as "a corollary to the income-absorption identity" and note both "always hold, but they do not provide an explanation."
