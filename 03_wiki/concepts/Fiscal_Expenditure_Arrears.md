## Overview

**Expenditure arrears** occur when a government fails to meet its expenditure commitments on time — typically interest payments or supplier invoices — accumulating unpaid obligations. Arrears are a form of **hidden fiscal deficit**: they reduce the measured cash deficit while the true resource cost (accrual deficit) remains unchanged. [RAW-BOOK Ch.3, Box 3.3, p.67]

## How It Works

The key distinction is between two deficit measures:

**Accrual deficit**: records the government's full obligations as they arise — including interest as it accrues, regardless of whether cash is paid. This is the true measure of the government's net use of resources.

**Cash deficit**: records only actual cash payments made. If the government delays interest payments or supplier payments, the cash deficit is smaller than the accrual deficit — but the unpaid obligations accumulate as arrears.

Arrears create two types of inconsistency: [RAW-BOOK Ch.3, Box 3.3]

1. **Interest arrears**: unpaid interest may be capitalized — added to the outstanding debt stock. This understates the accrual deficit in the current period but increases future debt service obligations.

2. **Expenditure arrears to suppliers**: the government receives goods and services but does not pay. This understates both the current deficit and the government's true claim on real resources. In GFS, these should be recorded when the obligation arises (accrual basis), not when paid.

Arrears are particularly common in transition and developing economies where governments face cash shortfalls but cannot formally declare a budget overrun. They represent a **quasi-invisible financing instrument** — effectively forcing suppliers and creditors to involuntarily lend to the government. ## Evidence and Sources

[RAW-BOOK Ch.3, Box 3.3, p.67] Ouanes and Thakur: "Arrears reflect a government's inability to meet its expenditure commitments — often interest payments — on time and result in serious inconsistencies between the accrual and cash deficits. The accrual deficit, which shows the government's net use of resources, reflects the government's full interest obligations, while the cash deficit reflects actual interest payments."
