## Conceptual Foundation
The classification of currency (banknotes) as a liability on the Federal Reserve's balance sheet is both a legal mandate (Section 16 of the Federal Reserve Act) and an economic necessity based on the definition of banking [RAW-CLIP]. As a central bank is fundamentally a bank, any note or deposit it issues is an IOU, regardless of whether there is a specific asset for which it can be "redeemed" [RAW-CLIP].

## The Taxation Logic
The most mechanistic explanation for why currency is a liability of the issuer (the Fed/Treasury) and an asset to the holder is its role in tax settlement:
1. **Tax as Liability:** Taxes due to the government are a liability for the public and an asset for the Treasury.
2. **Settlement Asset:** When a citizen pays $10 in currency to settle a $10 tax bill, the public's tax liability is extinguished.
3. **Issuer Liability:** For the $10 in currency to cancel a $10 asset (tax claim) on the Treasury's books, the currency itself must have been a liability of the Treasury/Fed system [RAW-CLIP].

## The Currency Franchise
Similar to a commercial bank's "deposit franchise"—the value derived from providing payment and safety services to depositors—the central bank maintains a **currency franchise** [RAW-CLIP].
- **Service Provision:** Currency provides a public service (medium of exchange, finality of settlement).
- **Revenue Generation:** This franchise generates a stream of revenue for the central bank (seigniorage), which conceptually makes it look like an asset, but in accounting terms, it remains a liability that supports the bank's intermediation role [RAW-CLIP].

## Counter-Arguments and Nuance
Some economists (like Randy Wright) challenge the "liability" designation as purely semantic if the note cannot be redeemed for anything other than another note [RAW-CLIP].
- **"Thanks Sucker" Note:** If a note says "thanks" but is accepted for taxes, it becomes a liability only because the issuer accepts it as a discharge of a debt owed to them [RAW-CLIP].
- **Public Asset:** From a public service perspective, currency could be viewed as a "public asset," though this does not change its position on the T-account [RAW-CLIP].
