## Overview

**Seigniorage** is the real revenue a government earns from money creation — the purchasing power appropriated by expanding the monetary base. The **inflation tax** is the corresponding real loss imposed on holders of domestic currency as inflation erodes their purchasing power. The two are equal when the public keeps real cash balances constant; in general, seigniorage equals the inflation tax plus the change in real money demand. [RAW-BOOK Ch.3, Box 3.1, p.64]

Seigniorage can be decomposed into two parts:
1. **Pure seigniorage**: revenue from real growth in money demand (new money absorbed without price increase)
2. **Inflation tax component**: revenue from reducing the real value of existing money balances

## How It Works

**The inflation tax is highly inefficient.** The cost inflicted on the public significantly exceeds the revenue collected by the government for two reasons: [RAW-BOOK Ch.3, Box 3.1]

First, seigniorage is shared: banks also collect part of it (by borrowing cheaply from the CB), and may redistribute it to borrowers via below-market loan rates. The government only captures a fraction of total seigniorage.

Second, **base erosion**: as inflation rises, the public reduces real cash balances by substituting foreign currency (dollarization) and other inflation hedges. This shrinks the tax base — so the government must push inflation still higher to maintain the same revenue, creating a self-reinforcing dynamic.

**Inverted-U (Laffer) curve**: seigniorage revenue rises with inflation up to a maximum, then falls as base erosion accelerates. Beyond the peak, higher inflation yields less revenue — the government is on the wrong side of the curve. Maximum seigniorage in industrial countries is typically estimated at 1% of GDP; in high-inflation developing economies it can temporarily reach 5–10% of GDP before base erosion sets in. [RAW-BOOK Ch.3, Box 3.1]

**Link to fiscal deficit**: seigniorage is one of four ways to finance a deficit (alongside domestic borrowing, foreign borrowing, and privatization). It is the implicit "inflation tax" route — invisible in the GFS fiscal accounts but captured in the monetary survey as expansion of reserve money.
