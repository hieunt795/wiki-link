## The Credibility Leeway in Supply Shocks

Supply shocks (e.g., energy price surges due to geopolitical conflict) present a fundamental trade-off for central banks: growth versus inflation. The capacity to "look through" such shocks (not responding with rate hikes) is functionally dependent on the **anchoring of inflation expectations** [RAW-CLIP].

- **High Credibility Scenario**: If inflation expectations are well-anchored, the CB has the policy space to tolerate temporary inflation spikes above target. This avoids a contractionary response that would further damage growth [RAW-CLIP].
- **Low Credibility / Jumping Expectations**: If the public or markets perceive a risk that inflation will remain high, inflation expectations may jump. In such cases, the CB may feel compelled to react (hike) even if the shock is transitory, simply to send a signal of determination [RAW-CLIP]. As President Lagarde (March 2026) noted: "the public may find it difficult to understand a reaction function that does not react" [RAW-CLIP].

## 2026 Case Studies: Fed, ECB, and BoJ

As of May 2026, the major central banks illustrate different facets of this mechanism:

- **ECB (High Credibility but Signaling)**: Despite having high anti-inflationary credibility, a jump in inflation expectations in early 2025 led the ECB to signal hikes to prevent "scarring" from previous late responses (e.g., in 2022). The ECB's posture became reactive, prioritizing the signaling effect to maintain credibility [RAW-CLIP].
- **Fed (Uncertainty & Reaction Function)**: The Fed faced a potential loss of credibility as inflation reached 3% and the leadership transitioned to Kevin Warsh. Dissenting hawkish voices in May 2026 reflected a desire to put the incoming chair on notice against damaging the Fed's hard-won credibility through premature easing [RAW-CLIP].
- **Bank of Japan (The Price of Lost Credibility)**: The BoJ illustrates the cost of a policy framework lacking credibility. In May 2026, Japan spent approximately $34.5 billion (JPY 5.4 trillion) on FX intervention to support the yen — an attempt to substitute FX intervention for rate hikes necessitated by a lack of fundamental policy credibility [RAW-CLIP]. FX intervention cannot compensate for the absence of a credible monetary and fiscal framework [RAW-CLIP].

## Summary

Credibility is not just a reputational asset; it is a functional component of the **policy reaction function**. It determines whether a central bank can prioritize growth during a supply shock or must pivot to inflation defense to prevent a de-anchoring of expectations. [LLM]
