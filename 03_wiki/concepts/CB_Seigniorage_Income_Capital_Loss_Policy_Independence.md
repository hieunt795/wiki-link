## Seigniorage: The Main Source of CB Income

Seigniorage = income earned on assets backing CB liabilities, minus the cost of issuing those liabilities.

**Banknotes**: Zero-interest liability; physical cost of production (≈3 pence per note, BoE 2006) is trivial relative to face value. Income earned = return on backing assets. [RAW-CLIP]

**Reserves**: No physical production cost, but often interest-paying liabilities. If remunerated at policy rate, seigniorage from reserves is reduced or eliminated. [RAW-CLIP]

**CBs with liquidity shortage** earn positive seigniorage: banknotes are a large, zero-cost liability; lending to commercial banks earns repo rates or market returns. [RAW-CLIP]

**CBs with liquidity surplus** often earn negative income: absorption operations pay out interest on CB-issued securities; assets (FX reserves, monetised government debt) earn below-market rates. [RAW-CLIP]

## Do Central Bank Losses Matter?

**Theoretical answer**: No — because a CB can always create domestic currency to meet its domestic liabilities. Many CBs have operated with negative capital (Bindseil et al. 2004). [RAW-CLIP]

**Empirical answer**: Yes — correlation exists between CB losses/poor capitalisation and poor policy outcomes including high inflation (Stella 2010; Dalton and Dziobek 2005). Poorly capitalised CBs may loosen policy to avoid further losses for reputational or political economy reasons. [RAW-CLIP]

## The Reputational Constraint (Buiter 2008)

The binding limit: when accumulated CB losses exceed the net present value of future seigniorage income, the CB cannot credibly commit to future obligations. This triggers:
1. Loss of public confidence in currency
2. Agents refuse to hold domestic liabilities
3. Demand for reserves-to-banknote conversion spikes → inflation spiral [RAW-CLIP]

The tipping point is not a hard accounting threshold but the moment the general public loses confidence in the CB's ability to service future obligations. [LLM]

## Policy-Social Welfare Trade-off

CB social objectives differ from profit maximisation:
- QE: buy bonds at low yield (high price) during crisis → sell at higher yield (loss) when economy recovers → socially optimal despite financial loss
- This is the intended design of a QE program; the loss confirms the program worked [RAW-CLIP]

Former BoE Deputy Governor Bean: "The aim of quantitative easing... is to help the MPC achieve its macroeconomic objective... The accounts of the APF are not designed to address the overall macroeconomic costs or benefits." [RAW-CLIP]

## Optimal CB Capital Level

No universal formula: depends on institutional structure, operations, and risk exposure (Cukierman 2010; Derbyshire 2010). Unlike private banks, CBs:
- Face no regulatory capital requirements
- Cannot readily access external capital markets (wholly government-owned in most cases)
- Must weigh capital adequacy against the need for operational and policy independence [RAW-CLIP]
