## Context: What Incomes Policy Is and Is Not

**Incomes policy** refers to direct government interventions in wage and price formation — distinct from demand management (fiscal/monetary policy). It targets:
- The **inertial component** of inflation: inherited expectations perpetuated through wage contracts, indexation clauses, and price-setting conventions
- The **wage-price spiral** channel: wage increases → cost push → price increases → renewed wage demands

"Inertial inflation is inherited, very often as a result of past excess demand inflation or exogenous price shocks that have been perpetuated through contracts and wage commitments (including indexation)." [RAW-BOOK IMF Macro p.4867]

**What incomes policy cannot do:** Eliminate excess demand inflation (requires fiscal/monetary tightening). Controls imposed without demand management generate shortages and black markets.

---

## Three Motivations for Incomes Policy

Particularly in transition economies, incomes policies serve three distinct objectives [RAW-BOOK IMF Macro p.986]:

```
1. Break inertial inflation
   Mechanism: Inflation expectations embedded in wage contracts perpetuate
              price growth even after demand shocks are removed.
              Wage controls can break the wage-price-exchange rate spiral
              when monetary/fiscal tightening alone cannot.

2. Prevent decapitalization of state-owned firms
   Mechanism: In transition, uncertain ownership + soft budget constraints
              create incentives for SOE managers to extract resources via wages.
              Wage norms prevent insolvency-threatening wage escalation
              before privatization establishes market discipline.

3. Strengthen credibility of exchange rate anchor
   Mechanism: Fixed exchange rate programs require wage restraint to prevent
              real appreciation. Wage controls provide forward guarantee that
              domestic costs will not undermine the peg.
```

## Three Institutional Approaches

### Approach 1 — Guideline Approach
Government sets explicit numerical norms for permissible wage and price growth:
```
Form: "Nominal wages may not grow faster than X% in period T"
      → typically tied to productivity growth target

Conventional derivation of wage guideline:
  IF income shares (wages/non-wages in GDP) remain stable:
  Permissible nominal wage growth = labor productivity growth rate

Enforcement: Voluntary (moral suasion) or legislated
Weakness: No automatic penalty for non-compliance
```

### Approach 2 — Social Contract
Tripartite agreement between government, employers, and labor:
```
Mechanism: Collective negotiation → agreement on wage growth ceiling
           + reciprocal government commitment (e.g., price restraint, tax relief)

Strength: Legitimate; builds consensus; durable if trust maintained
Weakness: Breaks down under inflationary pressure; requires functioning
           institutional infrastructure (unions, employer federations)
           → Absent in early-stage transition economies
```

### Approach 3 — Tax-Based Incomes Policy (TIP)
Financial penalties (or rewards) for exceeding (or meeting) wage norms:
```
Excess Wage Tax:
  IF enterprise pays wages above the norm → pay punitive tax on excess wages
  Polish case (popiwek): Tax rates escalated steeply above the norm
  → Disincentive to grant inflationary wage increases

Reward TIP:
  Firms that stay within norms receive tax relief or subsidized credit

Advantages over guidelines:
  → Automatic enforcement (tax system, not ad hoc monitoring)
  → Applies symmetrically across enterprises
  → Provides price signal, not quantity constraint
```

## Four-Step Design in Transition Economies

Designing wage controls in transition requires explicit choices on four dimensions [RAW-BOOK IMF Macro p.1001]:

### Step 1 — Selecting the Wage Norm
Two dominant norm types, each with distinct incentive properties:

```
WAGE BILL NORM
  Definition: Total wage bill (payroll) may not grow faster than norm rate
  Formula: ΔW_bill ≤ n% × W_bill_previous

  Advantages:
    + Enterprises retain flexibility to set relative wages (high-skill premium)
    + Administrable through tax system (based on payroll taxes)
    + Creates incentive to shed low-productivity workers (labor discipline)

  Disadvantage:
    - May accelerate unemployment when enterprises cut workers to stay
      within payroll limit

AVERAGE WAGE NORM
  Definition: Average wage per employee may not grow faster than norm rate
  Formula: ΔW_average ≤ n% × W_average_previous

  Advantages:
    + Removes incentive to reduce headcount (employment stability)
    + Simpler to communicate and monitor

  Disadvantage:
    - Incentive to hire low-wage, low-skill workers to lower the average
      (dilutes skill mix)
    - Does not encourage worker shedding → suppresses productivity gains
```

### Step 2 — Indexation Mechanism
The critical tradeoff between anti-inflation function and real wage protection:

```
FULL BACKWARD INDEXATION
  → Wages automatically adjust to past inflation
  → Cannot act as nominal anchor (inflation perpetuates automatically)
  → Fails to break inertial inflation

NO INDEXATION
  → Maximum anti-inflation effect
  → May require unacceptably large real wage cuts → political/social resistance

PARTIAL FORWARD-LOOKING INDEXATION (compromise adopted by most)
  → Wages indexed to projected (program) inflation, not actual past inflation
  → Cushions real wage loss IF program delivers on inflation targets
  → Breaks backward-looking expectations: agents must believe in the program
  → Poland 1990 model: partial indexation against projected not actual CPI
```

### Step 3 — Coverage
Which entities are subject to controls?

```
ARGUMENT FOR UNIFORM COVERAGE (public + private):
  Anti-inflationary effectiveness requires symmetry across sectors.
  Selective coverage creates arbitrage: workers migrate to uncovered sector.

ARGUMENT FOR SOE-ONLY COVERAGE:
  Root problem is SOE governance failure, not private sector behavior.
  Extending controls to private enterprises imposes costs on efficient firms.

IMF perspective: In transition economies, the justification for controls is
concentrated in SOEs (soft budget constraints, uncertain ownership, managerial
incentives to extract wages). Private enterprises facing hard budget constraints
have natural incentives for wage restraint.
```

### Step 4 — Enforcement
How are controls actually implemented?

```
EXCESS WAGE TAX (most common in transition):
  → Punitive tax on wages above the norm
  → Administered through existing payroll tax system
  → Effective without requiring enterprise-level monitoring teams

SOCIAL PACT:
  → Tripartite agreement replacing formal legal mandate
  → Relies on social consensus and union discipline
  → More fragile but avoids distortions of tax-based approach

MONITORING + SANCTIONS:
  → Inspectors + fines (traditional command-economy approach)
  → High administrative cost; prone to corruption
  → Being abandoned in transition reforms
```

## Historical Application: Poland 1990 Heterodox Program

Poland's 1990 stabilization combined three mutually reinforcing elements:
```
1. Tight fiscal/monetary policy      → Remove excess demand
2. Fixed exchange rate peg           → Nominal anchor for expectations
3. Tax-based incomes policy          → Break wage-price-exchange rate spiral

The combination was termed "somewhat heterodox": orthodox in its demand
management, heterodox in adding the exchange rate anchor and wage controls.
```

**Popiwek (Excess Wage Tax — EWT) mechanics** [RAW-BOOK IMF Macro p.1052]:
```
1990: Initial norm = wage bill; switch to average wage norm in 1991
Indexation: 60% of CPI inflation (partial forward-looking scheme)
Coverage: Public enterprises only; private firms (>50% private ownership) exempt

EWT rate structure (progressive):
  Excess 0–3% above norm:    100% tax on excess
  Excess 3–5% above norm:    200% tax on excess
  Excess >5% above norm:     500% tax on excess
  → Rate reduced to 300% maximum by 1993

Interaction: EWT + high unemployment + low enterprise profits + hard budget
             constraints all reinforced each other in holding down wages
             → IMF notes difficulty isolating EWT's independent contribution
```

Result: Monthly inflation fell from 55% (October 1989) to near 5% by end-1990.
The popiwek (excess wage tax) played a documented role in slowing nominal wage growth during the critical first year. [RAW-BOOK IMF Macro p.1021, p.1052]

## Effectiveness and Limits

Historical evidence from IMF programs reveals an asymmetric effectiveness pattern:

```
EARLY PHASE:
  → Effective: Controls complement demand restraint by pre-anchoring expectations
  → Can deliver sharper disinflation than demand management alone
  → Buys time for credibility to build

LATER PHASE:
  → Effectiveness diminishes rapidly as:
    - Enterprise managers learn evasion mechanisms
    - Controls create inter-firm and inter-sector wage distortions
    - Political pressure builds for exemptions and adjustments
    - Real wages erode → social resistance

IMF institutional position (1995):
  "Support for wage controls in IMF-supported adjustment programs has been
   limited. In general, such controls are approved as temporary measures in
   heterodox stabilization programs and in transition economies when a compelling
   argument can be made that their benefits outweigh the costs." [RAW-BOOK IMF Macro p.1013]
```

**Key design principle:** "The challenge to policymakers is therefore to ensure that wage controls are temporary and to minimize the distortions that inevitably result." [RAW-BOOK IMF Macro p.1013]

## Diagnostic

```
SIGNAL: CPI inflation persisting despite tight monetary/fiscal policy
→ Likely source: inertial component (contract indexation, expectations)
→ Consider: incomes policy as complement to demand restraint

SIGNAL: SOE wages accelerating while enterprises are insolvent/loss-making
→ Governance failure mechanism: soft budget constraint + uncertain ownership
→ Direct need: wage norm specifically targeting SOE sector

SIGNAL: Wage controls in place but inflation not decelerating
→ Coverage problem: uncovered sectors bidding up wages → migration
→ Or: indexed to past inflation → not breaking backward-looking expectations
→ Or: enforcement weak → evasion via fringe benefits, bonuses

SIGNAL: Wage controls maintained beyond 12-24 months
→ Warning: distortions accumulating → relative wage rigidity
→ Time to exit: replace with hard budget constraints + enterprise reform
```
