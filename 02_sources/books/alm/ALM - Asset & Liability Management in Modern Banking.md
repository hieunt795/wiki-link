#### **Executive Summary**

*Asset & Liability Management in Modern Banking: Theory, Applications, and Case Studies* explores the profound transformation of balance sheet management in an era of monetary instability, regulatory tightening, and digital acceleration.

Bridging academic rigor and practical insight, the book examines how Asset & Liability Management (ALM) has evolved from a traditional risk-measurement discipline into a strategic intelligence function at the heart of modern financial institutions.

The first part of the volume develops a comprehensive theoretical and methodological framework:

- Interest rate risk, convexity, and basis exposure;
- Behavioral modelling of non-maturing deposits and prepayment risk;
- Integration of ALM with liquidity and capital management;
- The role of hedge accounting (IFRS 9) and macro fair value hedging in stabilizing earnings and regulatory capital.

The second part applies these concepts through empirical case studies and scenario simulations, drawing on recent market events — from the Global Financial Crisis to the 2023 U.S. regional bank turmoil.

Each case illustrates how ALM functions can identify vulnerabilities, design effective hedging structures, and optimize balance sheet resilience across rate cycles.

Throughout, the book emphasizes that effective ALM is not about forecasting, but about preparing — through data-driven, scenario-based, and governance-integrated frameworks that align profitability, liquidity, and stability.

Combining analytical depth with real-world relevance, *Asset & Liability Management in Modern Banking* serves as both a reference manual for practitioners and a didactic foundation for scholars and policymakers seeking to understand how banks can transform balance sheet risk into strategic opportunity in the next generation of finance.

# **PART I Theory and Framework**

#### **Chapter 1 – Introduction to Asset & Liability Management**

#### **1.1 Rationale and Definition of ALM**

In the contemporary banking environment, **Asset & Liability Management (ALM)** represents a core strategic discipline that bridges the gap between risk management, balance sheet optimization, and long-term profitability. ALM entails the integrated management of a financial institution's assets (e.g. loans, securities) and liabilities (e.g. deposits, borrowings) so as to maintain stability in earnings, capital, and liquidity under variable market conditions.

As defined in the literature, ALM is "the process of formulating, implementing, monitoring, and revising strategies relating to assets and liabilities to achieve an institution's financial objectives, given its risk tolerances and constraints" (Romanyuk, *Asset and Liability Management: An Overview*, 2010). This holistic view contrasts with fragmented "siloed" approaches that treat interest rate risk, liquidity risk, and funding risk separately.

The keyword in ALM is **matching**—that is, aligning the timing, cash flows, and interest rate sensitivity of assets and liabilities. Poor matching exposes the bank to mismatch losses when interest rates shift or funding stress emerges.

In its broadest sense, ALM lies at the intersection of **strategy, markets, and regulation**. While Treasury and market risk functions may address short-term trading risks, ALM's purview is structural and long-term: steering the bank's core balance sheet through evolving environments.

#### **1.2 Objectives of ALM**

The primary objectives of an ALM function within a bank can be summarized as follows:

#### 1. **Interest Rate Risk Mitigation**

- o Control the sensitivity of net interest income (NII) and economic value of equity (EVE) to changes in the interest rate environment.
- o Prevent adverse effects from parallel or non-parallel rate shifts, curve twists, and volatility changes.

#### 2. **Liquidity & Funding Reliability**

- o Ensure that the institution can meet its cash obligations under normal and stressed conditions (e.g. deposit withdrawals, maturities).
- o Safeguard liquidity buffers and stable funding sources (e.g. retail deposits, secured funding, wholesale markets).

#### 3. **Capital Preservation & Return Optimization**

- o Safeguard the bank's capital base by avoiding undue mark-to-market losses and by adhering to regulatory constraints (e.g. Basel, ICAAP, IRRBB).
- o Seek to optimize spread income, risk premia, and ALM contributions to the return on regulatory capital.

#### 4. **Regulatory Compliance & Governance**

- o Comply with supervisory requirements (e.g. IRRBB guidelines, liquidity mandates, stress testing).
- o Provide transparent reporting and risk oversight to the ALCO, Board, and regulatory bodies (for example, as recommended in the PRA's "Asset and Liability Management: Suggestions for Greater Effectiveness").

#### 5. **Strategic Steering & Scenario Readiness**

- o Use scenario analysis and stress testing to anticipate adverse shocks (e.g. rate regime shifts, funding liquidity stress).
- o Adjust balance sheet posture proactively rather than reactively.

These objectives must be achieved under realistic constraints: capital adequacy, regulatory limits, internal risk appetite, and funding availability.

#### **1.3 The ALM Function and Organizational Placement**

The effectiveness of ALM hinges on both technical models and sound governance. Key organizational elements include:

- **ALCO / Treasury Committee**: A senior forum (often composed of CEO, CFO, CRO) that defines ALM strategy, sets limits, and reviews performance. The PRA supervisory guidance suggests that the ALCO should be forward-looking and empowered to deliberate both historic results and scenario-driven decisions. [bankofengland.co.uk](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/supervisory-statement/2013/asset-and-liability-management-suggestions-for-greater-effectiveness.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
- **ALM Office / Desk**: Executes the day-to-day modeling, scenario simulations, positioning, and reporting.
- **Risk & Finance Interface**: Tight integration with risk (market, credit, liquidity) teams, as well as finance/accounting (especially for hedge accounting and valuation).
- **Governance & Escalation**: Clear lines of authority, documented minutes, exception reporting, and alignment with ICAAP / ILAAP.

In many banks, ALM is embedded in a broader **Total Bank Management (TBM)** function, which encompasses not only interest rate and liquidity risk but also strategic decisions on credit, capital allocation, and business lines.

#### **1.4 Key Concepts and Metrics**

To pave the path for subsequent chapters, here are foundational concepts and common metrics:

- **Repricing Gap / Maturity Gap**: The difference between assets and liabilities that will reprice or mature within a given horizon.
- **Duration Gap**: A more sophisticated measure that weights cash flows by time and discounting, estimating interest rate sensitivity of equity (e.g. duration (assets) – (liabilities × assets/liabilities ratio)).
- **ΔEVE (Change in Economic Value of Equity)**: The projected change in the net present value of the bank's assets minus liabilities under a rate shock.
- **ΔNII (Change in Net Interest Income)**: The expected variation in interest income minus interest cost over a horizon (often 1 year) under different rate scenarios.
- **Liquidity Gap & Cash Flow Mismatch**: Assessing when inflows and outflows may create funding stress.
- **Behavioral Modeling & Non-Maturing Balances**: Recognizing that not all deposits behave according to contractual maturities, particularly demand or savings accounts.

Later chapters will explore **convexity risk, vega sensitivity, hedge accounting**, and **optimization frontiers**, building upon these basic building blocks.

#### **Chapter 2 – Balance Sheet Dynamics and Interest Rate Sensitivity**

#### **2.1 The Structure of the Bank Balance Sheet**

The banking balance sheet reflects a fundamental economic transformation: the conversion of **short-term liabilities** (such as customer deposits or wholesale funding) into **long-term earning assets** (such as loans and securities). This maturity transformation, while essential to intermediation, creates exposure to changes in interest rates and liquidity conditions.

#### A simplified structure may be represented as follows:

| Assets                                           |     | Liabilities & Equity              |     |
|--------------------------------------------------|-----|-----------------------------------|-----|
| Loans to customers (short- and long-term)        | 60% | Customer deposits (demand & term) | 70% |
| Investment portfolio (sovereign, corporate, ABS) | 20% | Wholesale funding (repos, bonds)  | 15% |
| Interbank placements                             | 10% | Central bank operations           | 5%  |
| Cash and reserves                                | 5%  | Equity capital                    | 10% |

This schematic balance sheet underscores the structural asymmetry between **asset maturity** (longer, fixed-rate) and **liability maturity** (shorter, floating-rate). It is precisely this asymmetry that ALM seeks to manage.

As the Bank for International Settlements (BIS, *Sound Practices for Managing Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book*, 2016) notes, "banks' earnings and economic value are vulnerable to changes in interest rates when the maturity or repricing structure of their assets, liabilities, and off-balance-sheet positions are mismatched."

#### **2.2 Repricing and Duration Gaps: Core Analytical Tools**

#### **(a) The Repricing Gap**

The **repricing gap** measures the difference between the volume of assets and liabilities that reprice within a specified time bucket. It is typically used to estimate *short-term* exposure to interest rate changes.

Repricing 
$$Gap(t) = RSA(t) - RSL(t)$$

#### Where:

- **RSA** = Rate Sensitive Assets
- **RSL** = Rate Sensitive Liabilities

If the repricing gap is positive, assets reprice faster than liabilities — the bank benefits from rising rates but suffers when rates fall. Conversely, a negative gap implies vulnerability to rising rates.

Although straightforward, this method fails to account for timing and magnitude differences in cash flows — hence the need for a more refined approach.

#### **(b) The Duration Gap**

The **duration gap** captures the *weighted average maturity* of assets and liabilities, providing a measure of how the bank's net worth responds to interest rate shocks:

$$DG = D_A - \frac{L}{A}D_L$$

where:

- = duration of assets
- = duration of liabilities
- /= leverage ratio (liabilities over assets)

A positive duration gap indicates that the economic value of equity (EVE) will fall when interest rates rise (since assets are longer). A negative gap indicates the opposite.

Empirically, a 1% (100 bps) parallel rise in interest rates leads to an approximate change in EVE given by:

$$\Delta EVE = -DG \times A \times \Delta i/(1+i)$$

This measure is fundamental to the **IRRBB (Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book)** supervisory framework defined by the Basel Committee (BCBS #368, 2016; BCBS #496, 2021).

#### **2.3 Economic Value vs. Earnings Perspective**

There are two complementary perspectives to assess interest rate risk:

| Perspective                          | Horizon                           | Measure                               | Objective                             |
|--------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Earnings Perspective<br>(ΔNII)       | Short-term (1–2 years)            | Change in Net Interest<br>Income      | Assess impact on profitability        |
| Economic Value<br>Perspective (ΔEVE) | Long-term (entire cash flow life) | Change in Economic<br>Value of Equity | Assess impact on capital/equity value |

While the **ΔNII** perspective focuses on *income volatility*, the **ΔEVE** perspective captures *valuation risk*. Together, they provide a holistic picture of how sensitive the bank is to changes in the yield curve.

For example, under the Basel standardized 200 bps shock, a ΔEVE exceeding 15% of Tier 1 capital is considered an outlier and subject to supervisory scrutiny (EBA/GL/2018/02).

#### **2.4 Non-Linearity and Convexity in the Banking Book**

Duration assumes a linear relationship between rates and value changes. However, this is only a first-order approximation. In reality, bond and loan values exhibit **convexity**, meaning that the response of EVE to large rate movements is non-linear.

Convexity becomes critical when:

- Interest rate shocks are large (≥ 200 bps);
- Option-like features exist (caps/floors in loans, prepayment options, callable funding);
- Behavioral elements (non-maturing deposits) introduce asymmetry.

Banks with material option risk must complement duration measures with **effective duration** and **convexity adjustments** (see: Doff, *Risk Management for Financial Institutions*, Wiley, 2022).

#### **2.5 Behavioral Modeling: Non-Maturing Deposits (NMDs)**

One of the most complex elements in ALM is modeling **non-maturing deposits (NMDs)** retail or corporate balances with no contractual maturity, such as current accounts or savings accounts.

While these deposits can, in theory, be withdrawn on demand, empirical studies show that only a portion behaves as "core," stable funding. The behavioral duration of NMDs often ranges from **1 to 5 years**, depending on rate sensitivity and customer base (ECB, *Report on IRRBB Modelling Practices*, 2019).

Key modeling assumptions include:

- **Elasticity to interest rates** (how much deposit volumes change with rate moves);
- **Decay rate** (speed of outflow);
- **Pass-through rate** (extent to which deposit rates adjust to market rates);
- **Segmentation** by customer type (retail vs. corporate).

Incorrect modeling of NMDs was among the key weaknesses identified during the **U.S. regional bank crisis (2023)**, where institutions underestimated behavioral repricing and outflow risks.

#### **2.6 From Measurement to Strategic Positioning**

ALM is not only a measurement exercise — it is a **strategic management process**. The **duration gap** and **repricing gap** are diagnostic tools; the real decision lies in how management acts upon them.

Possible strategic stances include:

- **Defensive (short duration)**: limit exposure when rates are expected to rise.
- **Neutral (duration-matched)**: maintain balanced sensitivity.
- **Proactive (long duration)**: extend duration when rate cuts are expected.

In practice, banks combine *hedge accounting*, *derivative overlays* (e.g., IRS, swaptions), and *portfolio reallocation* (e.g., from FVOCI to HTC) to implement such strategies. The trade-offs between capital impact, liquidity, and earnings stability are central to ALCO decision-making.

#### **2.7 Managerial Insight: ALM as Strategic Control**

A well-calibrated ALM framework transforms risk measures into actionable intelligence. The interaction between ΔEVE, ΔNII, and regulatory capital forms the bank's *strategic frontier* — a trade-off between resilience and profitability.

Understanding this frontier enables the ALCO to decide whether to absorb, transfer, or hedge structural risks.

### **Chapter 3 – Interest Rate Risk Models and Measurement Frameworks**

#### **3.1 Introduction**

Interest rate risk (IRR) in the banking book (IRRBB) arises when changes in market interest rates affect a bank's earnings or economic value.

The management of IRRBB is central to Asset & Liability Management (ALM) since it influences both short-term profitability and long-term solvency.

The **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS 368, 2016; BCBS 496, 2021)** defines IRRBB as "the current or prospective risk to both the bank's capital and earnings arising from adverse movements in interest rates that affect the banking book positions."

Measurement frameworks must, therefore, capture both:

- **Earnings-based risk**, usually quantified as the change in *Net Interest Income (ΔNII)*; and
- **Value-based risk**, captured by the change in *Economic Value of Equity (ΔEVE)*.

These two dimensions jointly represent the bank's sensitivity profile to yield curve shocks.

#### **3.2 Conceptual Framework**

The objective of IRR measurement is to assess how interest rate shocks — parallel or nonparallel — affect:

- **Earnings** (through repricing of assets/liabilities and behavioral factors), and
- **Economic Value** (through discounted present value of cash flows).

#### **Dual Perspective of Interest Rate Risk**

| Dimension                  | Focus            | Horizon   | Primary Metric | Purpose                 |
|----------------------------|------------------|-----------|----------------|-------------------------|
| Earnings Perspective       | Profitability    | 1–2 years | ΔΝΙΙ           | Income stability        |
| Economic Value Perspective | Capital adequacy | Long-term | ΔΕVΕ           | Equity value resilience |

An integrated ALM framework models both views jointly, allowing the ALCO to optimize between short-term performance and long-term capital stability.

#### **3.3 Economic Value of Equity (ΔEVE)**

The **Economic Value of Equity** represents the net present value (NPV) of all future cash flows expected from on- and off-balance-sheet positions:

$$EVE = PV(Assets) - PV(Liabilities)$$

A change in interest rates alters these present values. The **ΔEVE** is computed as:

$$\Delta EVE = EVE_{shock} - EVE_{base}$$

where ℎrepresents the valuation under the stressed yield curve scenario.

#### **Supervisory EVE Scenarios (Basel/EBA)**

Under the standardized Basel IRRBB framework, banks are required to assess ΔEVE under six prescribed yield curve shocks:

| Scenario       | Description                     | Туре            |
|----------------|---------------------------------|-----------------|
| +200 bps       | Parallel upward shift           | Steepening      |
| –200 bps       | Parallel downward shift         | Flattening      |
| +Short / –Long | Short rates up, long rates down | Short steepener |
| -Short / +Long | Short rates down, long rates up | Short flattener |
| +Short only    | Short-term increase             | Twist           |
| –Short only    | Short-term decrease             | Twist           |

The **Supervisory Outlier Test (SOT)** flags banks with ΔEVE > 15% of Tier 1 capital as materially exposed (EBA/GL/2018/02).

#### **3.4 Net Interest Income (ΔNII)**

The **ΔNII** measure assesses the short-term earnings sensitivity to interest rate movements, typically over a 12-month horizon.

Unlike ΔEVE, it reflects **cash flow mismatches** rather than valuation effects.

$$\Delta NII = NII_{shock} - NII_{base}$$

ΔNII captures how much the bank's net interest margin (NIM) would change given a rate shock, considering:

- Timing of repricing for variable-rate instruments;
- Behavioral assumptions (e.g., prepayments, deposit betas);
- Optionality embedded in assets and liabilities.

ΔNII is crucial for understanding *profit resilience* — particularly relevant for banks heavily reliant on maturity transformation.

#### **3.5 Duration and PV01 Approaches**

The **Duration-based approach** is a first-order approximation of ΔEVE:

$$\frac{\Delta EVE}{EVE} \approx -DG \times \Delta i$$

where **DG (Duration Gap)** is defined as in Chapter 2.

However, for practical computation, many institutions employ the **PV01** (*Price Value of a Basis Point*) — the change in the present value of a position for a 1 bp change in yield:

$$PV01 = \frac{d(PV)}{d(i)} \times 0.0001$$

For portfolio-level aggregation:

$$\Delta EVE = \sum_{i} PV01_{i} \times \Delta i$$

The PV01 approach is used in both internal ALM models and regulatory stress tests as a more granular, instrument-level sensitivity measure.

#### **3.6 Simulation and Dynamic NII Modelling**

While static gap and duration models provide useful snapshots, **dynamic simulation models** offer a forward-looking perspective.

These models simulate future NII under various yield curve scenarios, behavioral responses, and balance sheet strategies.

A typical NII simulation framework includes:

- 1. **Baseline projection** (assuming stable balance sheet and rates);
- 2. **Interest rate scenarios** (parallel, non-parallel, stochastic);
- 3. **Behavioral adjustments** (loan prepayments, deposit outflows, pass-through rates);

4. **Strategic actions** (hedging, repricing, reinvestment strategies).

Dynamic simulations can be **deterministic** (based on fixed rate paths) or **stochastic** (Monte Carlo, Hull-White or CIR models), integrating volatility and convexity effects.

#### **3.7 Behavioral Modelling of Optionality**

Many banking book positions contain **embedded options**, such as:

- Fixed-rate loans with prepayment rights;
- Callable term deposits or wholesale funding;
- Caps and floors in variable-rate products.

Ignoring these features can materially distort both ΔEVE and ΔNII estimates. Hence, **effective duration** and **option-adjusted spread (OAS)** techniques are employed to adjust for embedded convexity.

Advanced approaches model these using **option pricing models** (e.g., Black, 1976; Hull, 2017) or **behavioral econometrics** (ECB, 2019).

The goal is to capture asymmetric sensitivity: for example, prepayment risk increases when rates fall, reducing effective duration.

#### **3.8 Stress Testing and Scenario Analysis**

Regulatory frameworks require banks to conduct **regular IRR stress tests**, exploring extreme yet plausible rate movements.

A comprehensive stress-testing program should include:

- **Parallel and non-parallel shifts** (steepeners, flatteners, twists);
- **Basis risk scenarios** (e.g., widening between Euribor and OIS);
- **Behavioral stresses** (deposit outflows, accelerated prepayments);
- **Convexity shocks** (non-linear sensitivity to large rate changes).

Scenario design should be consistent with the bank's **Risk Appetite Framework (RAF)** and **Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP)**.

For instance, under a +200 bps parallel shock, a bank with a DG = 0.5 years and EVE of €10bn would experience:

$$\Delta EVE = -0.5 \times 10,000 \text{ million} \times 0.02 = -100 \text{ million} \text{ EUR}$$

This ΔEVE would represent a 1% reduction in EVE, well within regulatory thresholds — but the same shock could imply a ΔNII deterioration depending on asset-liability composition.

#### **3.9 Integration with Risk Governance and ALCO Decision-Making**

Quantitative results from IRR models must be interpreted within the governance structure. ALM Committees (ALCO) use these outputs to define **strategic duration targets**, **hedging strategies**, and **funding mix** adjustments.

#### **Integration steps include:**

- Mapping ΔEVE/ΔNII outputs to risk appetite thresholds;
- Evaluating hedge effectiveness under IFRS 9;
- Linking ALM metrics to ICAAP and ILAAP;
- Setting transfer pricing mechanisms reflecting duration and liquidity costs.

Effective governance ensures that IRR measurement is not a compliance exercise but a strategic lever influencing **pricing, funding, and investment**.

#### **3.10 Emerging Topics: Convexity Risk and Non-Linear Exposures**

Recent crises (e.g., the 2023 U.S. regional bank failures) have underscored the limits of linear IRR models.

**Convexity risk**, defined as the sensitivity of duration itself to rate changes, becomes material when yield volatility increases.

A practical market proxy for convexity exposure is **YIV-Beta** — the sensitivity of a bank's stock returns to changes in *yield implied volatility* (Cremers, Fleckenstein & Gandhi, 2021). Banks with high YIV-Beta exhibit stronger vulnerability to rate uncertainty, as in the case of **Silicon Valley Bank (SVB)** prior to its collapse.

Integrating convexity analysis into ALM frameworks — through stress testing of yield volatility and option-adjusted metrics — represents the frontier of interest rate risk management.

### **Chapter 4 – Liquidity Risk and Funding Strategy in ALM**

#### **4.1 Introduction**

Liquidity risk is one of the most fundamental components of the Asset & Liability Management (ALM) framework.

While interest rate risk and credit risk shape a bank's *profitability* and *valuation*, liquidity risk determines its *survival*.

As highlighted by the **Basel III framework** (BCBS 238, 2013), the 2007–2009 Global Financial Crisis revealed how the erosion of market liquidity and the over-reliance on short-term wholesale funding could trigger systemic crises even among solvent institutions.

Liquidity management in ALM involves balancing **liquid asset holdings** with **stable funding structures**, ensuring that the bank can meet its obligations as they come due — both in normal times and under stress.

This chapter explores the conceptual, quantitative, and strategic dimensions of liquidity risk management within modern banking, integrating regulatory and internal perspectives.

#### **4.2 Types of Liquidity Risk**

Liquidity risk manifests in multiple, interrelated forms:

| Туре                      | Definition                                                                            | Primary Drivers                                           |
|---------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Funding Liquidity Risk    | The inability to meet obligations when due without incurring unacceptable losses.     | Maturity mismatch, deposit volatility, market confidence. |
| Market Liquidity Risk     | The inability to liquidate assets without significant price discounts.                | Market depth, bid-ask spreads, asset concentration.       |
| Contingent Liquidity Risk | Unexpected cash outflows or loss of funding capacity due to off-balance-sheet events. | Credit line drawdowns, margin calls, reputational shocks. |

The ALM function typically focuses on **funding liquidity risk**, but must monitor market liquidity dynamics to ensure that balance sheet assets designated as "liquid" can indeed be monetized during stress periods.

#### **4.3 Liquidity Metrics: LCR and NSFR**

The Basel III liquidity standards established two complementary regulatory metrics to ensure both short-term and structural resilience:

#### **(a) Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)**

Ensures that banks maintain a sufficient stock of **High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA)** to cover **net cash outflows over a 30-day stress scenario**:

$$LCR = \frac{HQLA}{Net\ Cash\ Outflows_{30d}} \geq 100\%$$

#### **HQLA Composition:**

- Level 1 assets: cash, central bank reserves, sovereign bonds (0% haircut);
- Level 2A/2B: covered bonds, corporate debt, equities (subject to caps and haircuts).

The LCR enforces *short-term liquidity discipline*, limiting excessive maturity transformation.

#### **(b) Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)**

Promotes *medium-to-long-term structural stability* by requiring banks to maintain sufficient **Available Stable Funding (ASF)** relative to **Required Stable Funding (RSF)**:

$$NSFR = \frac{ASF}{RSF} \geq 100\%$$

**ASF Factors**: regulatory weights based on funding maturity and stability (e.g., retail deposits receive higher ASF factors than wholesale).

**RSF Factors**: weights reflecting the liquidity characteristics and residual maturity of assets.

The NSFR complements the LCR by discouraging structural reliance on short-term funding to finance illiquid assets.

#### **4.4 Internal Liquidity Metrics and ALM Dashboards**

Beyond regulatory ratios, banks employ a range of **managerial liquidity indicators** to capture nuances not covered by LCR/NSFR. Common examples include:

• **Cumulative Maturity Gaps**: projected net liquidity positions by time bucket;

- **Survival Horizon**: the number of days the bank can meet obligations under stress without external funding;
- **Liquidity Value-at-Risk (LVaR)**: probabilistic assessment of extreme liquidity shortfalls;
- **Collateral Utilization Ratio**: share of pledged vs. unencumbered assets;
- **Funding Concentration Indices**: Herfindahl-type measures for counterparty and source diversification.

These metrics feed into the **Liquidity Risk Dashboard**, a core ALM monitoring tool, linking realtime liquidity positions with stress test outputs and funding plan updates.

#### **4.5 Behavioral Modelling of Deposits and Funding Stability**

Accurate liquidity projections depend heavily on **behavioral modelling** — particularly for nonmaturing deposits (NMDs) and retail funding.

Unlike contractual maturities, behavioral profiles reflect depositor stability, sensitivity to interest rate changes, and stress elasticity.

#### **Key parameters include:**

- **Core vs. volatile deposits**: stable vs. rate-sensitive balances;
- **Deposit beta**: elasticity of deposit rates to changes in market rates;
- **Run-off rates**: modeled percentage of withdrawals under stress scenarios.

Empirical studies (e.g., ECB, 2019; EBA, 2020) show that stable retail deposits exhibit average maturities of 3–5 years in behavioral terms — substantially longer than contractual horizons which supports their treatment as "stable funding" within NSFR.

Behavioral assumptions are validated through *back-testing* and *stress recalibration* during periods of market volatility.

#### **4.6 Liquidity Stress Testing and Contingency Planning**

A robust liquidity management framework integrates **periodic stress testing** to assess the institution's ability to withstand idiosyncratic and systemic shocks.

#### **Core stress test scenarios include:**

- 1. **Institution-specific stress:** loss of wholesale funding, credit rating downgrade, or reputational damage.
- 2. **Market-wide stress:** systemic liquidity freeze, sudden interest rate shifts, collateral valuation shocks.

3. **Combined stress:** simultaneous idiosyncratic and market triggers.

The results feed into the **Contingency Funding Plan (CFP)**, detailing escalation procedures, funding sources, and governance actions (Board/ALCO triggers).

The CFP ensures readiness for liquidity crises by identifying *early warning indicators* (e.g., LCR < 110%, widening CDS spreads, deposit outflows) and pre-approved liquidity lines.

#### **4.7 Strategic Funding Management within ALM**

Liquidity management extends beyond compliance — it is a strategic driver of bank profitability and resilience.

#### **(a) Funding Mix Optimization**

The optimal funding mix minimizes cost while ensuring stability:

| Funding Source          | Advantages                  | Limitations                    |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Retail deposits         | Stable, low cost            | Limited scalability            |
| Wholesale funding       | Rapid access, flexible      | Volatile, confidence-sensitive |
| Covered bonds           | Term funding, lower spreads | Requires eligible collateral   |
| Securitizations         | Risk transfer               | Complexity, market dependence  |
| Central bank facilities | Liquidity backstop          | Stigma, policy dependency      |

A balanced funding structure aligns maturity profiles and limits reliance on short-term wholesale markets.

#### **(b) Transfer Pricing Mechanisms (FTP)**

Funds Transfer Pricing translates liquidity costs and benefits into product-level profitability. Modern FTP frameworks internalize:

- Cost of stable funding (NSFR-based premiums);
- Cost of liquidity buffers (LCR requirements);
- Term liquidity spreads (curve-dependent);
- Behavioral asymmetries (deposit stickiness vs. loan prepayment).

FTP alignment with ALM risk signals ensures coherence between *product pricing*, *liquidity risk*, and *strategic funding plans*.

#### **4.8 Integration of Liquidity and Interest Rate Risk**

Liquidity and interest rate risk are inherently interlinked.

An increase in market rates, for example, may reduce deposit stickiness (raising run-off risk), while liquidity stress can lead to forced asset sales that crystallize rate-driven valuation losses. The integration of both dimensions within the ALM governance cycle is thus essential.

#### **Analytical Linkages:**

- **Duration–Liquidity Trade-Off**: longer-term funding stabilizes NSFR but increases duration risk;
- **Liquidity–Spread Interaction**: tightening liquidity elevates funding spreads and depresses NII;
- **Asset Liquidity Premiums**: low-liquidity assets require higher returns, affecting portfolio optimization.

Modern ALM systems increasingly employ **Integrated Balance Sheet Simulators (IBS)**, which project EVE, NII, LCR, and NSFR jointly under dynamic scenarios — supporting holistic decision-making.

#### **4.9 Case Illustration: Managing Liquidity in a Rate-Hike Cycle**

Consider a mid-sized European bank with:

- LCR = 135%; NSFR = 110%;
- Behavioral core deposits = 70% of retail base;
- Funding gap at 6–12 months bucket = €1.2bn.

Facing a rapid tightening cycle, ALCO assesses two strategic responses:

| Option A                                             | Option B                                                  |
|------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Issue €2bn covered bonds at 5-year maturity          | Reprice retail deposits by +40 bps to retain funding base |
| Improves NSFR to 120%, but compresses NIM by 10 bps  | Preserves LCR buffer and stabilizes core deposits         |
| Higher term funding cost, lower liquidity volatility | Greater rate sensitivity, but flexible repricing          |

Decision: a **hybrid approach** combining partial covered bond issuance and selective retail repricing achieves both stability and profitability objectives.

#### **4.10 Governance, Policies, and Regulatory Expectations**

Effective liquidity risk management is underpinned by a strong **governance framework** integrating Board oversight, ALCO decision-making, and risk reporting.

According to **EBA Guidelines on Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP, 2019)**, key elements include:

- Defined **Liquidity Risk Appetite (LRA)** and tolerance limits;
- Periodic **ILAAP review** integrated with ICAAP;
- **Independent validation** of models and assumptions;
- Regular **Board reporting** and supervisory dialogue.

Supervisors emphasize consistency between liquidity, funding, and interest rate risk governance, particularly under **SREP (Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process)** assessments.

#### **4.11 Emerging Themes: Liquidity in a Digital and ESG Context**

Two structural trends reshape the liquidity landscape:

#### 1. **Digital Transformation and Deposit Volatility**

Mobile banking accelerates deposit mobility and amplifies "digital bank run" dynamics. ALM models must adapt behavioral parameters to shorter decision lags and social contagion effects.

#### 2. **ESG and Green Funding Instruments**

The rise of **Green Bonds** and **Sustainability-Linked Loans** introduces a new funding dimension — aligning liquidity profiles with sustainability targets. Investors increasingly reward institutions with transparent ESG-linked funding strategies, creating an intersection between *liquidity optimization* and *sustainable finance*.

#### **Chapter 5 – Balance Sheet Optimization and Strategic ALM**

#### **5.1 Introduction**

In modern banking, the **balance sheet is the core reflection of a bank's risk–return profile**. Each asset, liability, and off-balance-sheet item contributes to the institution's structural positioning toward interest rate, liquidity, and credit risks.

The **Asset and Liability Management (ALM)** function thus serves not only as a risk control mechanism but as a *strategic engine* that shapes the bank's profitability, capital efficiency, and resilience.

**Balance sheet optimization** seeks to identify the configuration of assets and liabilities that maximizes shareholder value—typically measured in terms of *risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC)* or *economic value added (EVA)*—while remaining compliant with internal and regulatory constraints such as capital, liquidity, and interest rate limits.

This chapter explores the analytical framework, quantitative tools, and governance processes that underpin balance sheet optimization in the ALM context, culminating in an integrated model of *Strategic ALM*.

#### **5.2 Objectives of Strategic ALM**

Strategic ALM integrates financial, regulatory, and economic dimensions into a coherent decision-making framework.

Its objectives can be summarized as follows:

- 1. **Optimize the structural balance sheet mix** to achieve a target risk–return trade-off;
- 2. **Ensure consistency with the bank's Risk Appetite Framework (RAF)** and regulatory requirements (ICAAP, ILAAP, IRRBB);
- 3. **Stabilize earnings and capital** under plausible macroeconomic and rate scenarios;
- 4. **Support strategic planning** by aligning funding, investment, and hedging strategies with medium-term business objectives;
- 5. **Facilitate ALCO decision-making** through quantitative dashboards and scenario simulations.

Ultimately, Strategic ALM transforms the balance sheet from a static construct into a dynamic portfolio, managed with the same sophistication as a financial investment fund but constrained by regulatory and fiduciary principles.



#### **5.3 The Optimization Problem in ALM**

At its core, balance sheet optimization can be represented as a **multi-objective constrained optimization problem**.

A simplified formalization is:

$$\max_{\mathbf{x}} \quad R(\mathbf{x}) - \lambda \cdot \sigma(\mathbf{x})$$
  $C(\mathbf{x}) \leq C_{min}$  (Capital constraints)  $CR(\mathbf{x}) \geq 100\%$  (Liquidity coverage)  $RSFR(\mathbf{x}) \geq 100\%$  (Stable funding)  $RRBB(\mathbf{x}) \leq limit$  (Interest rate risk limits)  $\sum_i x_i = 1$  (Balance sheet identity)

# Where:

- ()= expected return of balance sheet configuration ;
- ()= volatility (risk measure, e.g. NII or EVE volatility);
- = risk aversion coefficient;
- (), (), etc. are nonlinear constraint functions.

In practice, optimization models combine *stochastic simulations* (for interest rate, spread, and liquidity shocks) with *linear programming* or *Monte Carlo optimization techniques* to find efficient portfolios of assets and liabilities.

# **5.4 The Efficient Frontier in ALM**

The concept of the **Efficient Frontier**, derived from Modern Portfolio Theory (Markowitz, 1952), can be adapted to the banking context.

Here, the objective is not to maximize returns for a given asset portfolio, but to **maximize Net Interest Income (NII) or Return on Assets (ROA) for a given level of Interest Rate Risk (IRR)**.

# **Example representation:**

| Bank   | NII / Total Assets | ΔNII / Tier 1 | Positioning  |
|--------|--------------------|---------------|--------------|
| Bank A | 2.0%               | 4.5%          | Efficient    |
| Bank B | 1.6%               | 6.0%          | Over-risked  |
| Bank C | 1.9%               | 3.8%          | Balanced     |
| Bank D | 1.3%               | 2.5%          | Under-risked |

Plotting these data points in a two-dimensional space (Return vs. Risk) yields the **ALM Efficient Frontier**, identifying which configurations deliver the best trade-off between profitability and sensitivity.

Banks positioned below the frontier are *inefficient*, either taking too much risk for a given return or earning too little for their risk exposure.

This framework is instrumental for **ALCO decisions**, allowing management to benchmark its structural performance against peers or internal targets.

# **5.5 Risk–Return Trade-offs: NII, EVE, and Capital Sensitivity**

ALM optimization requires the simultaneous management of three key sensitivities:

| Dimension              | Measure       | Strategic Objective                  |
|------------------------|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| Short-term performance | ΔΝΙΙ          | Stabilize earnings under rate shocks |
| Long-term value        | ΔEVE / Tier 1 | Protect capital from rate shifts     |
| Capital efficiency     | RWA / Tier 1  | Maximize risk-adjusted return        |

The interplay between these factors defines the **risk–return map** of the balance sheet.

For instance, extending asset duration (longer fixed-rate loans or sovereign bonds) typically increases NII under rising rates but reduces EVE sensitivity and consumes more capital under IRRBB rules.

Thus, ALM optimization involves navigating *regulatory capital, liquidity buffers,* and *rate exposure* simultaneously.

#### **5.6 Integration with Capital and Liquidity Planning**

Effective balance sheet optimization requires consistent integration across:

- 1. **Capital Planning (ICAAP)** aligning IRRBB and market risk exposures with available capital buffers;
- 2. **Liquidity Planning (ILAAP)** ensuring funding stability and LCR/NSFR compliance under optimized scenarios;
- 3. **Strategic Planning** ensuring the ALM structure supports long-term profitability and growth.

This integration is operationalized through a **Strategic ALM Model (SALM)**—a multi-year projection tool that simulates NII, EVE, capital, and liquidity under macro scenarios, providing quantitative input to the bank's *Strategic Plan* and *Recovery Plan*.

#### **5.7 Quantitative Tools and Scenario Analysis**

Modern ALM optimization leverages advanced analytical tools, including:

- **Dynamic Balance Sheet Models (DBSM):** simulate future balance sheet evolution under macroeconomic scenarios (GDP, inflation, rates).
- **Interest Rate Scenario Engines:** generate stochastic rate paths consistent with yield curve dynamics and volatility structures (e.g., Nelson–Siegel models).
- **ALM Optimizers:** employ linear or stochastic programming to maximize NII subject to regulatory constraints.
- **Sensitivity Dashboards:** visualize ΔNII, ΔEVE, and risk-weighted asset changes under base and stress conditions.

These tools form the quantitative backbone of **data-driven ALCO decisions**, linking modeling outputs directly to strategic steering.

# **5.8 Governance and Decision Framework: The ALCO Role**

The **Asset & Liability Committee (ALCO)** is the decision-making body responsible for steering the balance sheet within the Risk Appetite and regulatory limits.

#### **ALCO responsibilities include:**

- Reviewing NII/EVE sensitivity results and approving hedging actions;
- Balancing short-term profitability against long-term stability;
- Approving funding and investment strategies;
- Coordinating with Treasury, Risk Management, and Finance functions;
- Monitoring regulatory metrics (LCR, NSFR, IRRBB).

ALCO effectiveness depends on **timely, integrated information** from ALM dashboards, ensuring strategic alignment between *risk measurement* and *business objectives*.

#### **5.9 Practical Case: Strategic Repositioning under Rising Rates**

Consider a commercial bank with:

- NII/Tot. Assets = 1.7%;
- ΔNII/Tier 1 = 7.0%;
- Duration gap = +0.25 years;
- SOT (Supervisory Outlier Test) NII = –7.5%.

Facing a rising rate environment, the ALCO explores two optimization strategies:

| Strategy               | Description                                   | Expected Effect                                       |
|------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| 1. Asset Reallocation  | Buy €2bn of 2-year sovereign bonds<br>(FVOCI) | +0.15% NII, improves ΔNII/Tier 1 to 6.3%              |
| 2. Derivative Strategy | Enter receiver IRS (€2bn, 2Y)                 | Lower $\Delta$ NII volatility, reduce EVE sensitivity |
| 3. Funding Adjustment  | Increase term funding share from 15% → 25%    | Improves NSFR to 115%, slight NIM compression         |

Result: The combined strategy enhances stability (lower ΔNII volatility) while preserving profitability and improving liquidity resilience.

This demonstrates how *ALM optimization* translates into tactical adjustments aligned with strategic goals.

# **5.10 Challenges and Future Directions**

The increasing complexity of financial systems introduces new challenges for ALM optimization:

- 2. **Behavioral Uncertainty:** deposit and loan behavior under stress remains difficult to model accurately.

- 4. **ESG and Green Transition:** balancing sustainability targets with risk–return optimization introduces new decision layers.
- 5. **AI and Machine Learning Integration:** emerging techniques can enhance scenario calibration and behavioral modelling but raise transparency concerns.

# **Chapter 6 – Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB)**

#### **6.1 Introduction**

**Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB)** represents one of the most critical components of a bank's overall risk profile.

Unlike trading book risk—typically marked to market and actively hedged—IRRBB originates from the *structural mismatch* between interest-sensitive assets and liabilities in the *banking book*, which are generally held at amortized cost.

Fluctuations in interest rates affect both:

- 1. **Earnings** through changes in *Net Interest Income (NII)*, and
- 2. **Economic value** through the impact on the *Economic Value of Equity (EVE)*.

Managing IRRBB is therefore essential to preserving both short-term profitability and long-term capital stability.

Under **Basel standards** and the **EBA Guidelines on IRRBB (2021)**, banks must identify, measure, monitor, and control their interest rate exposures, ensuring that they remain consistent with their **Risk Appetite Framework (RAF)** and capital adequacy requirements (*ICAAP*).

#### **6.2 Components of IRRBB**

According to the **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS, 2016)**, IRRBB can be decomposed into several sub-components, each reflecting a different source of rate sensitivity:

| Type of IRRBB    | Definition                                                            | Typical Drivers                                      |
|------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Gap risk         | Arises from mismatched repricing dates between assets and liabilities | Fixed vs. variable rate mix, maturity transformation |
| Basis risk       | Arises from imperfect correlation between different rate indices      | Euribor vs. EONIA/SOFR, different repricing bases    |
| Yield curve risk | Arises from changes in the slope or shape of the yield curve          | Steepening/flattening scenarios                      |
| Option risk      | Arises from embedded options in products                              | Loan prepayments, deposit early withdrawals          |
| Convexity risk   | Arises from non-linear sensitivity to large rate movements            | Mortgage portfolios, callable bonds                  |

Banks must quantify and manage all these components using both **economic value** and **earnings-based** approaches.

# **6.3 Measurement Approaches: EVE and NII**

#### **(a) Economic Value of Equity (EVE)**

The **EVE** represents the present value of expected future cash flows from assets, liabilities, and off-balance-sheet items.

Changes in EVE under parallel or non-parallel rate shocks indicate the *structural sensitivity* of the bank's capital to interest rate movements.

$$\Delta EVE = EVE_{shock} - EVE_{base}$$

EVE is particularly relevant for assessing **long-term interest rate exposure**. A large negative ΔEVE implies that the bank's economic value—and therefore its capital—is eroded by rate movements.

#### **(b) Net Interest Income (NII)**

The **ΔNII** metric measures the variation in projected NII over a one-year horizon under alternative rate scenarios (e.g., ±200 basis points).

$$\Delta NII = NII_{shock} - NII_{base}$$

Unlike EVE, NII focuses on the **short-term earnings impact** of rate shocks. A negative ΔNII under a rate increase typically signals that the bank has more rate-sensitive liabilities than assets—i.e., a *liability-sensitive balance sheet*.

#### **6.4 Regulatory Framework**

Under **Basel III** and **EBA Guidelines (EBA/GL/2021/10)**, banks are required to:

- 1. Measure ΔEVE and ΔNII under six standard rate shock scenarios (parallel up/down, steepener, flattener, short up, long up).
- 2. Report results both in absolute (€) and relative (% of Tier 1 capital) terms.
- 3. Identify "outlier banks" under the **Standardized Outlier Test (SOT)**:
  - o **EVE SOT threshold**: ΔEVE / Tier 1 ≤ –15%
  - o **NII SOT threshold**: ΔNII / Tier 1 ≤ –5% (non-binding but monitored)
- 4. Integrate IRRBB into ICAAP and stress testing frameworks.

These requirements aim to ensure **comparability, transparency, and supervisory consistency** across European institutions.

# **6.5 Duration Gap and Sensitivity Analysis**

The **duration gap (DGAP)** is a fundamental measure of a bank's exposure to interest rate risk, reflecting the weighted average repricing mismatch between assets and liabilities:

$$DGAP = D_A - \frac{L}{A}D_L$$

where:

- = duration of assets,
- = duration of liabilities,
- /= leverage ratio of liabilities to assets.

The change in EVE can be approximated using the duration gap:

$$\Delta EVE \approx -DGAP \cdot A \cdot \Delta y$$

where Δis the interest rate shock.

#### **Interpretation:**

- A *positive* DGAP → asset-sensitive → benefits from rising rates (EVE down, NII up).
- A *negative* DGAP → liability-sensitive → benefits from falling rates (EVE up, NII down).

# **6.6 Advanced Modelling: Optionality and Behavioral Adjustments**

In practice, IRRBB measurement must incorporate **behavioural assumptions** about:

- **Prepayment rates** on fixed-rate loans;
- **Early redemption** of term deposits;
- **Non-maturing deposit (NMD)** stability;
- **Loan repricing elasticity** to policy rates.

Banks use models such as:

- **Option-adjusted duration (OAD)** for portfolios with embedded options;
- **Replication portfolios** to model NMDs;
- **Monte Carlo simulations** for stochastic rate paths and optionality.

The inclusion of **convexity** and **Vega effects**—sensitivity to rate volatility—is increasingly important in stress scenarios, as shown during the 2023 U.S. regional bank crisis (Jiang et al., 2023).

#### **6.7 Strategic Implications and ALM Actions**

The ALCO uses IRRBB metrics to make **strategic decisions** in areas such as:

| Action Area          | Objective                           | ALM Levers                          |
|----------------------|-------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Repricing profile    | Adjust duration and sensitivity     | Asset mix, term funding, swaps      |
| Hedging              | Mitigate EVE/NII volatility         | IRS, swaptions, macro-FVH           |
| Investment portfolio | Optimize yield vs. sensitivity      | HTC vs. FVOCI allocation            |
| Funding              | Stabilize costs, manage optionality | Retail term deposits, covered bonds |
| Capital management   | Control IRRBB capital absorption    | ICAAP integration, risk transfer    |

These actions must be **quantitatively supported** through scenario simulations and consistently documented for accounting and regulatory purposes (IFRS 9 + EBA GL/2021/10).

Effective IRRBB governance relies on:

Emerging challenges and trends include:

# **Chapter 7 – Behavioral Modeling and Non-Maturing Deposits (NMDs)**

# **7.1 Introduction**

Among all the elements of interest rate risk management, the treatment of **non-maturing deposits (NMDs)** stands out as one of the most challenging.

NMDs—such as demand deposits, current accounts, and savings accounts—lack a contractual maturity. They can be withdrawn at any time, yet historically display high stability and predictable behavioural patterns.

This dual nature—*legally short-term but behaviourally long-term*—creates a modeling dilemma: how should banks assign a duration to an instrument that, in theory, can reprice daily, but in practice behaves like a multi-year liability?

The answer lies in **behavioural modeling**: a set of quantitative and econometric techniques that translate observed depositor behaviour into stable, risk-sensitive parameters for **interest rate risk**, **liquidity management**, and **fund transfer pricing (FTP)**.

Understanding and modeling NMDs accurately is essential for:

- **Interest Rate Risk in the Banking Book (IRRBB)** quantification (EVE and NII sensitivity);
- **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)** and **Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)** projections;
- **Hedge Accounting** effectiveness (especially macro-fair value hedges);
- **Strategic ALM positioning** and funding mix optimization.

# **7.2 Nature and Classification of Non-Maturing Deposits**

The European Banking Authority (EBA, *Guidelines on IRRBB and CSRBB*, 2021) distinguishes between two key categories of NMDs:

| Category       | Examples                                              | Typical Behavioural Features                                       |
|----------------|-------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Retail NMDs    | Current accounts, savings accounts held by households | Stable, low rate sensitivity, slow reaction to market rate changes |
| Corporate NMDs | Operational deposits of businesses, public entities   | More volatile, faster repricing, sensitive to opportunity costs    |

Retail deposits typically exhibit **longer behavioural durations** and **lower elasticity** to policy rates than corporate deposits.

Conversely, institutional and wholesale deposits are more rate-sensitive and require cautious modeling, especially under stress conditions.

#### **7.3 Behavioral Assumptions and Stability Analysis**

To model NMDs, banks must estimate three key behavioural parameters:

# 1. **Core vs. Non-Core Portion:**

The *core* portion represents stable deposits that are expected to remain even under adverse conditions.

The *non-core* portion is volatile and rate-sensitive, often withdrawn when market yields rise.

$$Core\ Ratio = \frac{Stable\ Balances}{Total\ NMDs}$$

#### 2. **Average Life (Behavioural Duration):**

This is the weighted average time until repricing, reflecting customer stickiness. Empirical studies show retail NMDs often have behavioural durations between **2 and 5 years**.

#### 3. **Rate Sensitivity (Elasticity):**

Measured as the deposit rate's responsiveness to changes in market rates:

Elasticity = 
$$\frac{\Delta r_{deposit}}{\Delta r_{market}}$$

Elasticities typically range from **0.1–0.3 for retail** and **0.5–0.8 for corporate** accounts.

#### **7.4 Modeling Techniques**

Modern ALM functions use a mix of **statistical**, **econometric**, and **machine learning** techniques to estimate behavioural parameters.

The most widely adopted methodologies include:

#### **(a) Time Series and Regression Models**

Linear regressions or ARIMA models capture the relationship between changes in deposit balances/rates and market variables (e.g. Euribor, GDP, unemployment).

#### **(b) Survival and Attrition Models**

Used to estimate expected life and withdrawal probabilities of deposits. They model customer behaviour as a time-to-event process (e.g., Cox proportional hazards models).

# **(c) Replication Portfolio Approach**

Perhaps the most operationally relevant for ALM, this method models the NMD portfolio as a synthetic combination of fixed-rate instruments that replicate its duration and repricing characteristics.

$$\text{Replicating Portfolio Value} = \sum_i w_i \times PV_i$$

where are portfolio weights assigned to benchmark bonds or swaps of different maturities.

#### **(d) Machine Learning Extensions**

Neural networks and random forests can capture non-linear relationships between deposit behaviour and macroeconomic drivers (inflation, unemployment, consumer confidence). However, regulatory acceptance remains cautious, emphasizing **interpretability and backtesting**.

#### **7.5 The Replication Portfolio Framework**

The **replication portfolio** remains the gold standard under EBA and Basel IRRBB guidance. It allows ALM desks to translate uncertain behavioural liabilities into *market-based instruments* that can be risk-managed and hedged.

# **Steps in Building a Replication Portfolio:**

- 1. **Segment deposits** (e.g., retail current, retail savings, corporate).
- 2. **Estimate behavioural duration** and **elasticity** for each segment.
- 3. **Construct a synthetic portfolio** of fixed-rate assets or swaps that replicate the expected cash flow pattern.
- 4. **Test performance** under multiple rate and liquidity scenarios.
- 5. **Update periodically** based on observed depositor behaviour.

This approach also enables accounting alignment under **macro fair value hedge accounting (FVH)**—since the replicated exposures can be designated as hedged items against interest rate swaps.

# **7.6 Stress Testing and Scenario Analysis**

Behavioural models must be complemented by **stress scenarios** to evaluate deposit stability under extreme conditions.

| Stress Driver            | Description                                    | Impact on NMDs                                               |
|--------------------------|------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Rate Shocks              | Sudden rate hikes or cuts                      | Core ratio declines as customers reallocate to term deposits |
| Liquidity Stress         | Market disruption or loss of confidence        | Spike in withdrawals, especially from corporate deposits     |
| Reputational Event       | Negative news or credit downgrade              | Accelerated outflows                                         |
| Digital Channel Dynamics | Faster transfer mechanisms increase volatility | Shorter effective duration                                   |

For regulatory purposes, the **Basel III liquidity framework** (LCR, NSFR) provides conservative stability assumptions that serve as lower bounds for model validation.

# **7.7 Integration with IRRBB and Liquidity Metrics**

Behavioural NMD modeling influences both **interest rate** and **liquidity risk** measures:

| Metric | Dependence on NMD Modeling                             | Effect of Higher Duration/Core<br>Stability |
|--------|--------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| ΔΕVΕ   | Longer behavioural duration increases EVE sensitivity  | Higher capital volatility                   |
| ΔΝΙΙ   | Greater stickiness stabilizes NII under rate shifts    | Lower earnings volatility                   |
| LCR    | Core deposits treated as stable funding                | Improves LCR denominator                    |
| NSFR   | Long-term stable deposits improve stable funding ratio | Enhances structural liquidity               |

Hence, NMD assumptions directly shape the bank's **ALCO decisions** and **ICAAP-ILAAP coherence**.

Supervisors expect banks to maintain robust model governance, including:

- **Regular backtesting** vs. actual deposit retention rates;
- **Change management and recalibration procedures**;
- **Documentation and transparency** of modeling techniques;
- **Integration into ICAAP and SREP reporting**.

EBA explicitly requires that behavioural parameters "reflect observed, stable customer behaviour and be consistent with macroeconomic conditions" (*EBA/GL/2021/10, para. 142– 150*).

#### **7.10 Strategic Implications for ALM**

The strategic relevance of behavioural modeling extends beyond compliance. It shapes:

- 3. **Hedging strategy calibration** (e.g., IRS tenor selection);
- 4. **Liquidity buffer optimization**;
- 5. **Capital planning and stress scenario design**.

Banks that master behavioural modeling can achieve a competitive advantage through:

• More accurate margin forecasting;

- Lower funding cost volatility;
- Optimized hedge efficiency;
- Improved strategic decision-making in ALCO forums.

#### **7.11 Conclusion**

Behavioral modeling of non-maturing deposits sits at the intersection of *quantitative finance, behavioural economics, and strategic ALM management*.

An accurate representation of deposit behaviour allows banks to:

- Align interest rate and liquidity risk measurement;
- Support hedge accounting designations under IFRS 9;
- Anticipate customer reactions to monetary policy changes;
- Strengthen resilience under stress conditions.

In an era of increasing digitalization and rate volatility, the challenge is to build behavioural models that are both **data-rich and adaptive**, integrating real-time behavioural signals with robust econometric foundations.

# **Chapter 8 – Liquidity Risk Management and Funding Strategy**

#### **8.1 Introduction**

Liquidity risk represents the potential inability of a financial institution to meet its obligations as they come due, without incurring unacceptable losses.

It is fundamentally distinct from solvency risk: while capital protects against *losses*, liquidity protects against *timing mismatches*.

The 2008 Global Financial Crisis revealed that **illiquidity can destroy otherwise solvent institutions** within days. Consequently, international regulation—particularly the **Basel III framework**—introduced quantitative liquidity standards (LCR, NSFR) and strengthened qualitative expectations for **Liquidity Risk Management Frameworks (LRMFs)**.

From an **Asset and Liability Management (ALM)** perspective, liquidity management complements interest rate and funding strategies, ensuring that the bank maintains a balanced, stable, and cost-efficient funding structure under both normal and stressed conditions.

#### **8.2 Types of Liquidity Risk**

Liquidity risk manifests through multiple channels. The main categories are:

| Туре                      | Definition                                                            | Examples / Indicators                       |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Funding Liquidity Risk    | Inability to obtain funding to meet obligations                       | Deposit outflows, loss of market access     |
| Market Liquidity Risk     | Inability to sell assets without significant price concessions        | Bond market illiquidity, repo haircuts      |
| Intraday Liquidity Risk   | Short-term liquidity gaps within a single day                         | Payment system delays, collateral transfers |
| Contingent Liquidity Risk | Potential future liquidity needs from off-<br>balance-sheet exposures | Loan commitments, derivatives margin calls  |

These risks are closely interconnected and require an integrated **Liquidity and Funding Risk Management Framework (LFRMF)**.

# **8.3 The Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)**

The **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)** ensures that banks hold an adequate buffer of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to survive a severe 30-day stress scenario.

$$\text{LCR} = \frac{\text{Stock of HQLA}}{\text{Total Net Cash Outflows over 30 days}} \geq 100\%$$

## **Key components:**

- **HQLA:** Cash, central bank reserves, government bonds, and other high-quality securities subject to haircuts.
- **Outflows:** Expected withdrawals from retail, corporate, and wholesale deposits.
- **Inflows:** Contractual cash inflows capped at 75% of outflows.

The LCR is calibrated to ensure that banks can withstand *idiosyncratic* and *market-wide* stress scenarios without external support.

#### **8.4 The Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)**

The **NSFR** complements the LCR by promoting stable funding over a one-year horizon.

$$NSFR = \frac{Available Stable Funding (ASF)}{Required Stable Funding (RSF)} \ge 100\%$$

- **ASF:** Equity, long-term liabilities, and stable deposits.
- **RSF:** Assets weighted by liquidity and maturity characteristics.

NSFR ensures that longer-term assets are funded by sufficiently stable liabilities, thus limiting excessive maturity transformation.

#### **8.5 Liquidity Stress Testing**

Stress testing forms the **core of the internal liquidity risk framework**.

Banks must design stress scenarios capturing institution-specific vulnerabilities and systemic crises.

| Stress Type   | Drivers                             | Expected Effects                                    |
|---------------|-------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| Idiosyncratic | Credit downgrade, reputational loss | Sudden retail/corporate outflows                    |
| Market-wide   | Financial crisis, policy tightening | Funding market closure, collateral devaluation      |
| Combined      | Interaction of both                 | Liquidity spiral, rising haircuts, asset fire sales |

Stress results feed into **contingency funding plans (CFPs)**, which outline mitigation measures and emergency funding sources.

#### **8.6 The Role of ALM in Liquidity Management**

ALM acts as the **bridge between liquidity and interest rate management**. Its strategic objectives include:

- 1. **Maintaining structural liquidity** through an optimal asset-liability maturity profile.
- 2. **Ensuring funding diversification** across instruments, tenors, and counterparties.
- 3. **Optimizing the liquidity buffer** composition to balance cost and usability.
- 4. **Integrating IRRBB and liquidity analytics** in unified scenario frameworks.
- 5. **Supporting regulatory ratios** (LCR, NSFR) while sustaining profitability.

The **Asset and Liability Committee (ALCO)** defines limits, monitors gaps, and approves funding and investment actions consistent with the bank's risk appetite.

#### **8.7 Funding Strategy and Composition**

An effective **funding strategy** relies on diversification and stability.

The mix between retail deposits, wholesale funding, and capital market instruments defines both **liquidity resilience** and **interest rate sensitivity**.

| Funding Source                     | Characteristics                           | Advantages                 | Challenges                     |
|------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Retail Deposits                    | Stable, low-cost,<br>behaviourally sticky | Improves LCR/NSFR          | Limited scalability            |
| Wholesale Funding                  | Interbank, repos, senior unsecured        | Large volumes, flexibility | Sensitive to market confidence |
| Covered Bonds /<br>Securitizations | Asset-backed                              | Collateralized, long-term  | Complexity, regulatory caps    |
| Central Bank Funding               | TLTROs, standing facilities               | Reliable under stress      | Temporary, policy-dependent    |
| Capital Markets                    | Senior/non-preferred debt, Tier 2         | Improves term structure    | Market access volatility       |

#### **8.8 Liquidity Gap and Maturity Mismatch Analysis**

Liquidity risk is monitored through **maturity gap profiles**, displaying net cumulative cash flows by time bucket.

| Time Bucket | Cumulative Gap (€bn) | Interpretation                           |
|-------------|----------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Overnight   | -2.0                 | Short-term dependency on interbank lines |
| 1–7 days    | -1.5                 | LCR critical zone                        |
| 1–3 months  | -0.5                 | Neutral funding                          |
| 3–12 months | +0.8                 | Medium-term surplus                      |
| >1 year     | +3.0                 | Structural funding buffer                |

ALM aims to maintain a **negative gap only in short horizons**, offset by the HQLA buffer, while preserving a **positive long-term structural position**.

#### **8.9 Integration of Liquidity and Interest Rate Risk**

Liquidity and IRRBB are inherently linked:

- **Short-term funding increases rate sensitivity**, as repricing occurs faster.
- **Long-term fixed funding stabilizes earnings**, but raises liquidity costs.
- **Optionality in deposits** creates both liquidity and rate uncertainty.

ALM must therefore evaluate **combined shocks**—e.g., rate hikes coinciding with funding stress—through integrated *EVE-NII-LCR* simulations.

#### **8.10 Example: Funding Optimization under Stress**

| Scenario                     | Funding Action                      | LCR Impact | NII Impact | Strategic Consideration                         |
|------------------------------|-------------------------------------|------------|------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Baseline                     | Balanced retail & wholesale mix     |            |            | Stable funding structure                        |
| Stress 1: Rate hike +100 bps | Shift to longer-term retail funding | +10%       | –15m       | Increases LCR, reduces margin                   |
| Stress 2: Market freeze      | Use ECB facilities and repo HQLA    | +20%       | –10m       | Preserves liquidity, increases collateral usage |
| Stress 3: Deposit outflow 5% | Issue covered bonds                 | +15%       | –8m        | Strengthens NSFR, increases duration            |

This demonstrates the **trade-off between liquidity protection and earnings stability**, a central theme in modern ALM.

#### **8.11 Contingency Funding Plan (CFP)**

Every institution must maintain a **CFP** detailing:

- Early warning indicators (market spreads, liquidity ratios);
- Escalation procedures and communication lines;
- Pre-arranged collateral and repo lines;
- Central bank facility access;
- Recovery options and prioritization.

The CFP is the operational backbone of **liquidity crisis management**, linking strategic planning with tactical response.

#### **8.12 Governance and Supervision**

Supervisory expectations (EBA/GL/2018/02) require:

- **Board-level oversight** of liquidity risk appetite;
- **Integrated ICAAP/ILAAP documentation**;

- **Independent liquidity risk monitoring**;
- **Internal pricing through FTP frameworks**;
- **Regular validation and backtesting** of liquidity assumptions.

The alignment between **liquidity strategy** and **capital planning** ensures consistency within the bank's *overall risk governance architecture*.

#### **8.13 Emerging Trends in Liquidity Management**

Future liquidity management will be shaped by:

- 1. **Digital transformation** real-time liquidity dashboards, intraday analytics.
- 2. **ESG-linked funding** green bonds and sustainable deposits influencing funding mix.
- 3. **Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs)** potential new liquidity channels.
- 4. **Integrated stress testing** joint simulation of market, liquidity, and interest rate risk.
- 5. **Behavioural analytics** AI-based monitoring of depositor behaviour and funding flows.

These evolutions redefine the boundaries of ALM, positioning liquidity as both a risk and a strategic resource.

#### **8.14 Conclusion**

Liquidity management is no longer a defensive discipline; it is a **strategic determinant of resilience and profitability**.

An optimized funding strategy allows banks to:

- Safeguard stability under stress,
- Support business growth,
- Manage interest rate sensitivity proactively, and
- Comply efficiently with regulatory standards.

In modern banking, **liquidity, funding, and interest rate risk** form an integrated triad: mastering their interaction defines the competitive edge of sophisticated ALM functions.

# **Chapter 9 – Funds Transfer Pricing (FTP) and Internal Capital Allocation**

# **9.1 Introduction**

Funds Transfer Pricing (FTP) lies at the heart of the **Asset and Liability Management (ALM)** framework.

It is the mechanism through which the **costs, benefits, and risks of funding and liquidity** are internally priced and transferred across a bank's business units.

While regulatory frameworks (Basel III, EBA SREP) require that risks be properly identified, measured, and capitalized, FTP ensures that these risks are **economically internalized**—that is, correctly reflected in internal performance metrics and product pricing.

An effective FTP system serves three core purposes:

- 1. **Pricing:** ensuring that internal product rates reflect funding, liquidity, and interest rate risk costs;
- 2. **Performance Measurement:** aligning profitability indicators with risk-adjusted returns (RAROC, RORWA);
- 3. **Strategic Steering:** supporting ALCO in resource allocation and balance sheet optimization.

# **9.2 The Rationale for FTP**

In a modern, diversified bank, different divisions—Retail, Corporate, Treasury—generate assets and liabilities with varying maturities, optionalities, and behavioural characteristics. Without FTP, product-level profitability would be distorted by central funding and interest rate movements.

FTP acts as an *internal market mechanism*, ensuring:

- **Neutrality to funding structure:** business lines are evaluated independently of the bank's external funding mix;
- **Alignment with ALM strategy:** promoting behaviours consistent with the bank's risk appetite (e.g., stable deposits, long-term funding);
- **Transparency and accountability:** business units understand the cost of liquidity and rate risk embedded in their portfolios.

# **9.3 FTP Framework Structure**

A comprehensive FTP framework typically includes three interacting components:

| Component                | onent Purpose                                                        |                       |  |
|--------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------|--|
| Base Transfer Rate (BTR) | Reflects the cost of term funding or investment for given maturities | ALM / Treasury        |  |
| Liquidity Premium (LP)   | Captures cost of illiquidity or funding asymmetry                    | ALM / Risk Management |  |
| Risk Adjustment (RA)     | Reflects optionality, behavioural, or embedded risk                  | ALM / Product Units   |  |

The sum of these components determines the **Internal Transfer Rate (ITR)**:

$$ITR = BTR + LP + RA$$

Each product's internal margin is then calculated as:

# **9.4 Base Transfer Rate (BTR)**

The BTR represents the **risk-free term structure** of interest rates at which the bank can fund itself or invest.

It is generally derived from the **swap curve** or **bank-specific funding curve**, and should reflect:

- The *currency* and *tenor* of the transaction;
- The *repricing profile* of the product (fixed or floating);
- The *hedging horizon* defined by ALM.

#### **Example:**

| Tenor    | Reference Rate (EUR Swap) | Base Transfer Rate (bps) |  |  |
|----------|---------------------------|--------------------------|--|--|
| 3 months | 3M Euribor                | 0                        |  |  |
| 1 year   | 3M Euribor + 20           | +20                      |  |  |
| 3 years  | 3M Euribor + 40           | +40                      |  |  |
| 5 years  | 3M Euribor + 60           | +60                      |  |  |

ALM updates the BTR curve periodically, ensuring internal consistency between product pricing and external market rates.

#### **9.5 Liquidity Premium (LP)**

The liquidity premium accounts for **structural funding imbalances** between assets and liabilities.

It reflects the cost (or benefit) of obtaining stable funding beyond the horizon of liquid markets.

Typical considerations include:

- Market liquidity conditions (repo spreads, bond bid-ask);
- The bank's funding plan and NSFR position;
- Behavioural stability of deposits;
- Regulatory liquidity buffers.

| Product                         | Liquidity Premium (bps) |
|---------------------------------|-------------------------|
| Retail mortgage (10Y)           | +30                     |
| SME loan (5Y)                   | +20                     |
| Corporate credit line (undrawn) | +40                     |
| Retail deposit (core, stable)   | -10                     |

Liquidity premiums are dynamic and may vary according to **ALCO decisions** and **stress testing outcomes**.

#### **9.6 Risk Adjustment (RA)**

Risk adjustments capture **embedded optionalities and behavioural uncertainties**, such as:

- Prepayment risk on loans and mortgages;
- Early withdrawal options on deposits;
- Rate floors and caps on floating instruments.

These adjustments are typically estimated using **option-adjusted spread (OAS)** models or behavioural simulations, and ensure that business units bear the economic cost of embedded risks they generate.

# **9.7 FTP for Product Segments**

FTP application varies by product family:

| Product Segment         | FTP Design                                               | Key Considerations  Prepayment optionality |  |
|-------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|--|
| Fixed-rate loans        | Transfer fixed BTR for loan maturity                     |                                            |  |
| Floating-rate loans     | Transfer rolling short-term BTR                          | Basis risk                                 |  |
| Term deposits           | Transfer matching BTR                                    | Early redemption                           |  |
| Current accounts (NMDs) | Apply behavioural FTP rate based on replication duration | Stability, core portion                    |  |
| Off-balance-sheet lines | Transfer liquidity cost only                             | Drawdown probability                       |  |

FTP thus operationalizes the behavioural modeling from Chapter 7, ensuring economic consistency across ALM components.

# **9.8 FTP and Interest Rate Risk (IRRBB)**

FTP interacts directly with **IRRBB metrics**.

By transferring a fixed-term BTR to lending and deposit businesses, the ALM unit isolates the **interest rate position** centrally in Treasury, which can then be **hedged through derivatives** or **structural positioning**.

#### This structure:

- Prevents uncontrolled mismatches at business-unit level;
- Concentrates rate risk where expertise and instruments exist;
- Simplifies hedge accounting (FVH or CFH frameworks).

In effect, **FTP is the operational backbone of IRRBB management**.

#### **9.9 FTP and Liquidity Risk**

FTP also embeds **liquidity incentives**:

- Business units funding long-term loans with short-term sources incur liquidity charges;
- Units raising stable deposits receive liquidity credits;
- Treasury acts as a *liquidity clearing house*, balancing internal flows and ensuring regulatory ratio compliance.

This creates an **internal market for liquidity**, promoting optimal maturity transformation within risk appetite limits.

#### **9.10 FTP and Performance Measurement**

FTP-adjusted profitability forms the basis for **risk-adjusted performance metrics** such as:

$$RAROC = \frac{\text{Net Income after FTP}}{\text{Economic Capital}}$$
 $RORWA = \frac{\text{Net Income after FTP}}{\text{Risk-Weighted Assets}}$ 

This allows consistent comparison of business lines, factoring both funding costs and risk consumption.

ALM thus becomes an enabler of **value-based management**, aligning capital allocation with strategic objectives.

#### **9.11 FTP Governance**

An effective FTP framework requires:

- **Clear governance** through ALCO and Treasury policies;
- **Transparent methodologies** shared with business units;
- **Regular recalibration** of curves and liquidity premiums;
- **Audit trail** and documentation for supervisory review (EBA/GL/2018/02).

Roles are typically divided as follows:

| Function            | Responsibility                                |
|---------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| ALM/Treasury        | Curve definition, calibration, aggregation    |
| Risk Management     | Model validation, liquidity assumptions       |
| Finance/Controlling | Performance reporting, profitability analysis |
| Business Lines      | Product pricing, behavioural input            |
| Internal Audit      | Independent review                            |

#### **9.13 Challenges and Evolving Practices**

Modern FTP frameworks face new challenges:

- **Digital transformation**: rapid deposit flows altering behavioural durations;
- **Regulatory scrutiny**: supervisors requiring transparency and consistency;
- **ESG integration**: incorporating sustainability premiums into funding spreads.

Advanced institutions are adopting **dynamic FTP models** that link transfer rates to market and liquidity indicators in real time.

#### **9.14 Strategic Role of FTP in ALM**

FTP transforms ALM from a compliance function into a **strategic steering mechanism**. Through it, the bank can:

- Optimize **risk-adjusted profitability** across portfolios;
- Align product pricing with **liquidity and rate risk constraints**;
- Incentivize **stable funding behaviours**;

In this sense, FTP is not merely a pricing tool—it is the **internal language of risk, liquidity, and value** within the bank.

# **Chapter 10 – Capital Management, ICAAP, and SREP Framework**

#### **10.1 Introduction**

Capital management represents the **ultimate layer of financial resilience and strategic steering** in a modern bank's balance sheet architecture.

While liquidity ensures short-term survival and ALM governs structural risks, **capital absorbs unexpected losses**, sustaining the bank's solvency, market confidence, and regulatory compliance.

Under the Basel III and Basel IV frameworks, and as refined by the **EBA's Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)**, banks are required to maintain sufficient capital to cover both **Pillar 1** (quantitative minimums) and **Pillar 2** (bank-specific) risks.

To ensure the adequacy of these buffers, institutions must develop a comprehensive **Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP)**, aligned with their risk appetite, strategy, and macro-financial environment.

Capital management thus integrates the **prudential**, **economic**, and **strategic** dimensions of bank management, bridging the regulatory view of solvency with the internal view of value creation.

#### **10.2 Objectives of Capital Management**

The objectives of an effective capital management framework include:

- 1. **Ensuring solvency** through the maintenance of adequate capital buffers relative to risks;
- 2. **Optimizing capital efficiency**, maximizing shareholder value within regulatory constraints;
- 3. **Supporting strategic growth**, by allocating capital to profitable and sustainable business lines;
- 4. **Maintaining market confidence**, through transparent reporting and stable capital ratios;
- 5. **Aligning with supervisory expectations**, as formalized in the SREP and the EBA's guidelines on ICAAP.

In this sense, capital management serves as both a *compliance function* and a *strategic management tool*.

#### **10.3 Regulatory Framework: Basel and SREP**

The regulatory capital framework is structured along three complementary pillars:

| Pillar   | Focus                                                                          | Regulatory Reference |
|----------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------|
| Pillar 1 | Minimum quantitative capital requirements (credit, market, operational risk)   | Basel III / CRR2     |
| Pillar 2 | Internal capital adequacy assessment (ICAAP) and supervisory evaluation (SREP) | EBA/GL/2022/11       |
| Pillar 3 | Disclosure and market discipline                                               | CRR2 Part Eight      |

The **SREP** (Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process) ensures that each institution's capital and liquidity positions are **commensurate with its risk profile**.

Supervisors assess the ICAAP and ILAAP, determine the **Pillar 2 Requirement (P2R)** and **Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G)**, and may impose qualitative measures where deficiencies are observed.

# **10.4 The Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP)**

The ICAAP is the **core tool of internal capital management**, integrating risk measurement, capital planning, and governance.

According to the EBA Guidelines (EBA/GL/2022/11), the ICAAP must be:

- **Forward-looking**, covering at least a three-year horizon;
- **Comprehensive**, encompassing all material risks (including interest rate, liquidity, concentration, and business risk);
- **Proportionate**, reflecting the size, complexity, and systemic relevance of the bank;
- **Integrated**, linking strategic planning, budgeting, and risk appetite.

The **ICAAP cycle** can be summarized as follows:

- 1. **Identification and quantification of material risks**;
- 2. **Assessment of internal capital needs** under baseline and stressed scenarios;
- 3. **Aggregation of capital requirements** and comparison with available capital;
- 4. **Capital planning**, including dividends, issuances, and contingency measures;
- 5. **Governance and approval** by the Board and ALCO.

# **10.5 Economic Capital and Risk Coverage**

While regulatory capital (Basel III) is based on standardized or model-based formulas, **economic capital** represents the *bank's internal assessment of capital required to cover unexpected losses at a chosen confidence level*.

Economic capital is typically estimated for each risk type:

| Risk Type                 | Measurement Method                                        | Confidence Level (Typical) |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------|
| Credit Risk               | Credit VaR, unexpected loss                               | 99.9%                      |
| Market Risk               | Historical or Monte Carlo VaR                             | 99%                        |
| IRRBB                     | $\Delta \text{EVE}$ sensitivity or economic value-at-risk | 99%                        |
| Operational Risk          | Loss distribution approach                                | 99.9%                      |
| Liquidity Risk            | Stress-based shortfall models                             | 99%                        |
| Business / Strategic Risk | Scenario-based qualitative methods                        | 95–99%                     |

The total economic capital is the **aggregated amount required to maintain solvency with a probability exceeding 99.9% over one year**, adjusted for diversification effects.

#### **10.6 Capital Planning and Projections**

Capital planning links **strategic objectives** with **capital availability and constraints**. The process involves forecasting:

- **Risk-weighted assets (RWA)** evolution;
- **Profitability (NII, trading, fees)**;
- **Dividend and distribution policies**;
- **Funding and leverage metrics**;
- **Stress scenarios** (macro and idiosyncratic).

# **Example: Capital Forecast Summary (3-Year Horizon)**

| Metric              | 2024  | 2025 (Base) | 2026 (Stress) |
|---------------------|-------|-------------|---------------|
| CET1 Ratio          | 14.3% | 13.5%       | 11.8%         |
| Leverage Ratio      | 5.2%  | 5.0%        | 4.7%          |
| Total Capital Ratio | 18.1% | 17.3%       | 15.5%         |
| RWA (EUR bn)        | 120   | 126         | 132           |
| Profit (EUR mn)     | 1,450 | 1,380       | 950           |

The ICAAP must demonstrate that even under **severe but plausible** stress, capital ratios remain above regulatory minima and internal thresholds.

#### **10.7 Integration with Risk Appetite and ALM**

Capital adequacy cannot be assessed in isolation—it must be **consistent with the bank's risk appetite and balance sheet structure**. ALM provides the link between **economic risk measures** (ΔEVE, ΔNII) and **capital requirements**.

#### For instance:

- **Interest rate risk (IRRBB)** impacts both net interest income and the economic value of equity;
- **Liquidity stress** affects funding spreads and solvency under market pressure;
- **Credit deterioration** impacts capital via higher risk weights and provisions.

Through ICAAP integration, ALM indicators translate into **capital consumption metrics**, enabling proactive management.

#### **10.8 ICAAP and SREP Interaction**

The **SREP** assesses both the *quantitative adequacy* and the *qualitative robustness* of the ICAAP. Supervisors evaluate:

- 1. The governance structure and Board involvement;
- 2. The methodological soundness of internal models;
- 3. The coherence between ICAAP outcomes and strategic planning;
- 4. The credibility of stress testing and recovery plans.

The result is a **supervisory decision** assigning:

- **Pillar 2 Requirement (P2R)** binding additional capital;
- **Pillar 2 Guidance (P2G)** non-binding buffer for stress resilience;
- **Qualitative measures** (e.g., governance improvements, model reviews).

# **10.9 Stress Testing in ICAAP**

Stress testing is a **core analytical pillar** of ICAAP, designed to assess the impact of adverse macroeconomic scenarios on capital adequacy.

Typical stress scenarios include:

- Sudden interest rate shocks (parallel and non-parallel);
- GDP contraction and credit spread widening;
- Liquidity dry-up or deposit outflows;
- Asset price shocks in sovereign or corporate exposures.

|-------------------|---------------|---------------------|----------------|
| Parallel +200 bps | -12.5%        | -4.3%               | -80 bps CET1   |
| Parallel –200 bps | +8.9%         | +3.1%               | +55 bps CET1   |
| Steepener         | -6.4%         | -2.7%               | –40 bps CET1   |

This linkage demonstrates how **ALM sensitivities feed directly into capital projections**, closing the loop between structural risk and solvency.

#### **10.10 Governance and Documentation**

EBA (EBA/GL/2022/11) emphasizes the need for a **robust governance framework**, covering:

- Clear **roles and responsibilities** (Board, Risk Committee, ALCO, CFO);
- **Formal documentation** of methodologies and assumptions;
- **Independent validation** by Internal Audit and Risk Control;
- **Regular back-testing** of projections and stress outcomes;
- **Comprehensive reporting** to management and supervisors.

The **Board of Directors** bears ultimate accountability for ICAAP adequacy and must approve the annual ICAAP report.

#### **10.11 Capital Allocation and RAROC Integration**

Capital allocation connects ICAAP outcomes to **business strategy and performance measurement**.

Using **Risk-Adjusted Return on Capital (RAROC)** or **Return on Risk-Weighted Assets (RORWA)** metrics, the bank ensures that scarce capital is deployed toward **value-generating activities**.

$$RAROC = \frac{\text{Risk-Adjusted Net Income}}{\text{Economic Capital}}$$
  $RORWA = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Risk-Weighted Assets}}$ 

The **ALCO** and **Strategic Planning Committee** use these metrics to:

- Identify underperforming portfolios;
- Reprice or reduce high-risk exposures;
- Support capital-efficient product design;
- Optimize business mix under regulatory constraints.

#### **10.12 Linkage Between ICAAP and ILAAP**

The **Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP)** complements ICAAP by ensuring that **liquidity and funding** risks are covered by adequate resources and governance. Both processes share:

- Common scenarios and stress assumptions;
- Joint governance under the ALCO;
- Consistent communication to the supervisor.

An integrated **ICAAP–ILAAP** view allows holistic management of **solvency and liquidity under stress**, which is central to EBA's supervisory philosophy.

#### **10.13 Emerging Trends in Capital Management**

Modern capital management must adapt to several evolving dynamics:

- **Basel IV implementation**, introducing output floors and revised credit risk models;
- **Climate and ESG risks**, increasingly incorporated into capital planning (ECB Guide 2022);
- **Macroprudential buffers** (CCyB, O-SII, SyRB) requiring forward-looking allocation;
- **Digitalization of stress testing** using machine learning for scenario design;
- **Integration of ALM and ICAAP data architectures**, enabling near-real-time capital forecasting.

#### **10.14 Conclusion**

Capital management, through ICAAP and its supervisory evaluation under SREP, represents the **culmination of the ALM process**.

It transforms balance sheet analytics—interest rate, liquidity, and funding—into strategic decisions on **risk appetite, capital allocation, and value creation**.

In a world of rising macro volatility, regulatory scrutiny, and digital transformation, the integration of **ALM, ICAAP, and capital strategy** is essential not only for compliance, but for **sustainable banking resilience**.

# **Chapter 11 – Liquidity Risk Management and ILAAP**

#### **11.1 Introduction**

Liquidity is the lifeblood of banking. Unlike solvency, which concerns the long-term ability to absorb losses, **liquidity risk** refers to a bank's short-term ability to meet its financial obligations as they come due, without incurring unacceptable losses or triggering a loss of market confidence. The **Global Financial Crisis (GFC) of 2007–2009** demonstrated that even solvent institutions can fail rapidly when liquidity dries up.

In the post-crisis regulatory framework, liquidity management has evolved from a purely treasury-driven operational concern to a central element of the **prudential supervision architecture**.

The **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)** and **Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)** introduced under the **Basel III framework (BCBS, 2013)**, together with the **Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP)** mandated by the **European Banking Authority (EBA, 2018)**, form the cornerstone of the modern regulatory liquidity regime.

#### **11.2 The Nature of Liquidity Risk**

Liquidity risk arises from the **maturity transformation function** of banks: borrowing short-term (typically via deposits and market funding) and lending or investing long-term.

It can be classified into two primary categories:

- 1. **Funding Liquidity Risk** The inability to meet payment obligations due to lack of funding sources (e.g., deposit outflows, maturing wholesale debt, margin calls).
- 2. **Market Liquidity Risk** The inability to sell assets at or near their fair value because of market illiquidity, widening bid-ask spreads, or limited depth in secondary markets.

The interaction between these two forms can create **liquidity spirals** (Brunnermeier & Pedersen, 2009), where funding stress forces asset sales, further depressing market prices and increasing collateral haircuts.

#### **11.3 Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP)**

The **ILAAP** is a key component of the **Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)**, introduced by the EBA to complement ICAAP (Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process). It requires banks to **self-assess** their liquidity risk management frameworks, ensuring that they

maintain adequate liquidity buffers and funding structures aligned with their risk profile and business model.

The **EBA Guidelines on ILAAP (EBA/GL/2016/10 and 2021 revision)** identify four core principles:

- 1. **Comprehensiveness** Coverage of all liquidity and funding risks, including intraday liquidity, contingent risks, and off-balance-sheet commitments.
- 2. **Proportionality** Tailoring the framework to the bank's size, complexity, and systemic relevance.
- 3. **Forward-looking orientation** Incorporation of stress testing and scenario analysis to anticipate liquidity stress events.
- 4. **Integration with governance and strategic planning** Alignment with the bank's business model, risk appetite, and ICAAP.

The ILAAP must be documented in a formal **ILAAP Report**, reviewed annually by senior management and the Board, and submitted to the supervisory authority (ECB or national competent authority) as part of the SREP package.

#### **11.4 Components of a Robust Liquidity Risk Framework**

A sound **liquidity risk management framework** integrates both **quantitative** and **qualitative** elements, typically structured as follows:

#### **11.4.1 Governance**

- Board and ALCO (Asset & Liability Committee) oversight.
- Clearly defined liquidity risk appetite and tolerance thresholds.
- Integration with capital planning and recovery/resolution frameworks.

#### **11.4.2 Measurement**

- Daily monitoring of LCR, NSFR, and internal liquidity ratios.
- Cash flow projections by currency, entity, and maturity bucket.
- Stress testing under idiosyncratic and systemic shocks.

#### **11.4.3 Monitoring Tools**

- Concentration limits on funding sources and counterparties.
- Collateral eligibility and encumbrance analysis.
- Early warning indicators (EWIs) for deposit outflows or market stress.

# **11.4.4 Contingency Funding Plan (CFP)**

- Escalation procedures and communication plans.
- Identification of available funding actions (e.g., repo, central bank facilities).
- Definition of liquidity triggers for activation.

#### **11.5 Stress Testing and Scenario Design**

Liquidity stress testing under ILAAP must incorporate both **idiosyncratic shocks** (e.g., rating downgrade, reputational event) and **systemic stress scenarios** (e.g., interbank market freeze, sovereign crisis).

#### Key elements include:

- **Time horizon**: typically 30 days (LCR) and 90 days for extended scenarios.
- **Granularity**: projection of contractual and behavioral cash flows.
- **Assumptions**: run-off rates, market haircuts, and central bank access.
- **Reverse stress tests**: identifying conditions under which the bank would fail liquidity requirements.

Results must feed into the **liquidity buffer calibration** and **funding strategy**, ensuring that management actions are realistic and timely.

## **11.6 Interaction between ILAAP and ICAAP**

While ICAAP focuses on **capital adequacy** and solvency under stress, ILAAP addresses **liquidity and funding adequacy**.

The two processes are complementary and increasingly integrated under supervisory expectations (ECB, *Guide to ICAAP and ILAAP*, 2020):

| Dimension    | ICAAP                       | ILAAP                               |
|--------------|-----------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Objective    | Ensure capital adequacy     | Ensure liquidity adequacy           |
| Risk focus   | Credit, market, operational | Liquidity, funding, concentration   |
| Time horizon | Multi-year (3–5 years)      | Short-to-medium term (1–2 years)    |
| Output       | Capital plan, risk appetite | Funding plan, liquidity buffer      |
| Stress tests | Capital depletion           | Liquidity outflows and funding gaps |

Integration ensures a **holistic balance sheet view**, where capital and liquidity interact dynamically. For instance, **capital erosion** reduces market confidence and funding access, while **liquidity stress** may force asset fire sales impacting capital ratios.

# **11.7 The Role of the ALCO in Liquidity Management**

The **Asset & Liability Committee (ALCO)** acts as the central governance body for balance sheet and liquidity management.

Its responsibilities include:

- Reviewing LCR/NSFR and internal liquidity ratios.
- Approving funding and investment plans.
- Ensuring consistency with risk appetite and regulatory expectations.
- Coordinating ICAAP/ILAAP integration.

ALCO's forward-looking decisions on duration, funding mix, and buffer composition directly shape the bank's liquidity resilience and profitability trade-offs.

#### **11.8 Key Supervisory Expectations and Best Practices**

According to the **EBA SREP methodology** and **ECB guidance**, best practices for liquidity management include:

- Maintaining an **adequate buffer** of HQLA calibrated to stress scenarios.
- Ensuring **diversified and stable funding**, limiting reliance on volatile wholesale markets.
- Monitoring **intraday liquidity** positions and collateral encumbrance.
- Conducting **regular independent validation** of liquidity models and assumptions.
- Establishing a **liquidity transfer pricing (LTP)** mechanism to allocate liquidity costs across business lines.

Supervisors increasingly assess banks' **liquidity governance maturity** using qualitative scores, considering the consistency of the ILAAP with the institution's actual risk profile and management practices.

# **11.9 Emerging Topics: Climate and ESG Liquidity Risks**

Recent regulatory developments (EBA Discussion Paper, 2023) highlight the potential liquidity implications of **climate-related and ESG risks**:

- Physical climate risks can impact asset valuations and collateral liquidity.
- Transition risks can affect funding stability in carbon-intensive sectors.
- Investors' and depositors' preferences may shift rapidly, creating new **reputational liquidity shocks**.

Banks are therefore expected to **integrate ESG considerations** into liquidity stress testing and ILAAP governance, particularly in the context of **climate scenario analysis** (ECB, *Guide on Climate-Related Risks*, 2021).

#### **11.10 Conclusion**

Liquidity risk management has evolved from a reactive treasury function into a **strategic and supervisory pillar** of bank resilience.

The ILAAP provides a structured, forward-looking framework to ensure that liquidity and funding risks are identified, measured, and managed consistently with the institution's risk appetite and business strategy.

Its integration with ICAAP allows banks to achieve a **comprehensive view of balance sheet adequacy**, bridging solvency and liquidity in a coherent governance system.

As financial markets evolve—particularly with digital transformation, ESG pressures, and the normalization of monetary policy—the **strategic management of liquidity** will remain central to the stability and competitiveness of modern banks.

# **Chapter 12 – Integration of ICAAP and ILAAP: The Holistic Balance Sheet View**

#### **12.1 Introduction**

The integration of **capital** and **liquidity adequacy frameworks** — the **Internal Capital Adequacy Assessment Process (ICAAP)** and the **Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP)** — represents one of the most critical developments in modern bank risk governance.

While these two processes were originally conceived as distinct supervisory requirements under **Pillar II of Basel III**, recent regulatory evolution and market experience have shown that **capital and liquidity risks are interdependent** and must be analyzed within a **unified balance sheet management framework**.

The **European Central Bank (ECB, 2020)** explicitly encourages banks to move toward **holistic balance sheet management**, in which ICAAP and ILAAP jointly form the foundation of the institution's **strategic planning**, **risk appetite**, and **stress testing**.

This chapter explores how banks can integrate the two frameworks effectively to ensure **resilience, consistency, and strategic coherence** across solvency and liquidity dimensions.

#### **12.2 Conceptual Interdependence between Capital and Liquidity**

Although ICAAP and ILAAP serve distinct prudential purposes, their **interdependencies** are numerous and significant:

| Dimension            | ICAAP Focus                                | ILAAP Focus                                 | Interconnection                                                                    |
|----------------------|--------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Objective            | Capital adequacy under stress              | Liquidity and funding adequacy              | Liquidity shocks can erode capital;<br>capital depletion weakens funding<br>access |
| Primary Risk Drivers | Credit, market, operational, concentration | Funding, refinancing, market liquidity      | Market stress transmits across both                                                |
| Time Horizon         | Medium- to long-term (3–5 years)           | Short- to medium-term (1–2 years)           | Strategic capital plan influences funding plan                                     |
| Management Tool      | Capital plan, ICAAP report                 | Funding and liquidity plan, ILAAP report    | ALM acts as the link between the two                                               |
| Supervisory Outcome  | Pillar II capital add-on                   | Liquidity guidance and qualitative measures | Both influence SREP scores and capital/liquidity buffers                           |

The interplay between solvency and liquidity becomes evident during **stress conditions**:

- A **liquidity shortage** may force asset sales at depressed prices, leading to **capital losses**.
- Conversely, **capital deterioration** undermines market confidence, restricting access to funding and triggering liquidity stress.

These dynamics reinforce the need for a **joint assessment and governance approach**.

#### **12.3 Regulatory and Supervisory Framework**

The **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS, 2014)** and the **EBA (2021)** both stress that ICAAP and ILAAP should be "**two pillars of a single institution-wide framework**" for ensuring sound internal management.

Key regulatory documents shaping this integration include:

- **ECB (2020)** *Guide to the Internal Capital and Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Processes (ICAAP and ILAAP)*
- **EBA SREP Guidelines (2018)** *Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process Framework*
- **BCBS (2019)** *Principles for the Sound Management of Liquidity Risk and Capital Planning*

Supervisors expect banks to demonstrate **cross-consistency** in their ICAAP and ILAAP documentation, particularly regarding:

- Scenario design and macroeconomic assumptions
- Management actions and contingency measures
- Alignment of stress testing horizons and methodologies
- Internal governance, data quality, and model validation

The **Supervisory Review and Evaluation Process (SREP)** integrates findings from both ICAAP and ILAAP to determine a bank's **overall SREP score**, which influences **Pillar II capital addons** and **liquidity guidance (P2G)**.

#### **12.4 Building an Integrated ICAAP–ILAAP Framework**

An effective integration approach typically involves three main stages:

# **(1) Strategic Alignment**

Both processes must stem from the bank's **business model** and **risk appetite framework (RAF)**.

This requires:

- Common macroeconomic and financial assumptions (e.g., interest rate paths, GDP growth, market volatility).
- Consistency between **capital planning** and **funding plans** over the strategic horizon.
- Incorporation of **ALCO decisions** into both ICAAP and ILAAP cycles.

#### **(2) Methodological Consistency**

The methodologies applied in the two frameworks must be internally coherent:

- **Stress testing** scenarios must use consistent drivers (e.g., rate shocks, credit spreads, deposit outflows).
- **Data architecture** must ensure shared sources and traceability of assumptions.
- **Risk aggregation** should allow mapping liquidity effects (e.g., funding cost increases) onto capital impacts (e.g., lower NII, MTM losses).

#### **(3) Governance Integration**

The **Board of Directors** and the **Risk Committee** should oversee both ICAAP and ILAAP jointly, ensuring:

- A single **reporting cycle** for both documents.
- Coherence between the **Risk Appetite Statement (RAS)** and **Capital/Liquidity Buffers**.
- Centralized ALM coordination and treasury–risk alignment.

This ensures that ICAAP and ILAAP are not siloed exercises but **mutually reinforcing management tools**.

#### **12.5 Role of Stress Testing in Integration**

Stress testing is the primary operational bridge between ICAAP and ILAAP. Under the **ECB's integrated stress testing expectations (ECB, 2021)**, banks must design scenarios that simultaneously capture:

- **Capital impacts** (e.g., credit losses, MTM valuation effects, RWA increases).
- **Liquidity impacts** (e.g., deposit withdrawals, market funding spread widening).

A combined stress test must illustrate how **liquidity shocks affect solvency**, and vice versa. For instance:

- In a **systemic stress scenario**, a widening of sovereign spreads can both increase RWA (capital effect) and reduce collateral value (liquidity effect).
- A **funding market freeze** can lead to higher reliance on central bank liquidity, increasing leverage and potentially lowering capital ratios.

These feedback loops require **dynamic balance sheet modeling**—often embedded within the bank's **ALM system**—to capture the time-phased evolution of both capital and liquidity metrics.

#### **12.6 Integration via ALM: The Balance Sheet Nexus**

**Asset & Liability Management (ALM)** acts as the operational platform for ICAAP–ILAAP integration.

Through ALM, a bank links **earnings, capital, and liquidity** via consistent modeling of balance sheet dynamics.

#### **Key mechanisms include:**

- **Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB)**: affects both NII (income perspective) and EVE (economic value perspective), bridging profitability and capital.
- **Funding strategy decisions**: determine liquidity buffer needs and impact funding costs, which in turn affect NII and capital generation.
- **Capital and liquidity stress tests**: use ALM data to simulate behavioral responses of assets and liabilities under macro stress.

Hence, ALM functions as both a **risk quantification engine** and a **strategic coordination mechanism**, ensuring coherence between ICAAP and ILAAP assumptions.

## **12.7 The Holistic Balance Sheet View**

The **holistic view** integrates capital, liquidity, and interest rate risk management into a **single economic model of balance sheet resilience**.

Conceptually, it links three key dimensions:

| Dimension                  | Indicator                               | Strategic Focus                                 |
|----------------------------|-----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Capital Adequacy           | CET1 Ratio, Total Capital Ratio         | Solvency, RWA efficiency                        |
| Liquidity Adequacy         | LCR, NSFR, Internal Liquidity<br>Ratios | Short-term resilience, funding stability        |
| Interest Rate Risk (IRRBB) | ΔΝΙΙ, ΔΕVΕ                              | Earnings stability, economic value preservation |

The holistic balance sheet framework allows management and supervisors to evaluate the **trade-offs** between these dimensions.

#### For example:

- A **larger liquidity buffer** improves LCR but may reduce profitability.
- Extending **asset duration** increases NII sensitivity and EVE volatility, affecting both capital and liquidity usage.
- Increasing **capital buffers** enhances solvency but may limit lending and liquidity creation.

Thus, the **integrated ICAAP–ILAAP model** provides a coherent decision-making framework for balancing resilience and profitability.

#### **12.8 Supervisory Expectations for Integration**

The **ECB's 2020 ICAAP/ILAAP Guide** outlines explicit expectations for integration, emphasizing:

- 1. **Consistency in management actions** across capital and liquidity domains.
- 2. **Common scenario frameworks** for macroeconomic, market, and idiosyncratic stresses.
- 3. **Shared governance** via ALCO and the Risk Committee.
- 4. **Cross-validation** between ICAAP and ILAAP assumptions (e.g., dividend policy, funding costs, recovery triggers).

In recent **SREP cycles**, supervisors have begun explicitly assessing the **degree of integration** between the two frameworks, assigning higher qualitative scores to banks demonstrating joint stress testing and management reporting.

# **12.9 Integration Challenges**

Despite regulatory encouragement, many banks face practical challenges in achieving full integration:

- **Data fragmentation** between Treasury, Risk, and Finance functions.
- **Different time horizons** and modeling techniques (static vs dynamic).
- **Inconsistent governance cycles** and documentation timelines.
- **Complexity of behavioral modeling**, particularly for non-maturing deposits and contingent liquidity.

Leading institutions have responded by developing **integrated capital and liquidity dashboards**, **ALM-driven simulation engines**, and **joint ICAAP–ILAAP Committees** to ensure coordination and model transparency.

### **12.10 Future Developments: Toward an Integrated Resilience Framework**

The next evolution in prudential supervision is the convergence of ICAAP and ILAAP into a **single "Internal Resilience Assessment Framework" (IRAF)**, as discussed in ECB technical workshops (2023–2024).

This would formally integrate solvency, liquidity, and business model risk assessments within one strategic process, with:

- Unified scenario architecture.
- Joint reporting to the Board and supervisors.
- Quantitative resilience indicators combining capital, liquidity, and profitability metrics.

This vision underscores the shift from regulatory compliance toward **true strategic risk integration** across the entire balance sheet.

#### **12.11 Conclusion**

The integration of ICAAP and ILAAP represents the culmination of modern bank risk governance — moving from siloed risk management toward a **unified, forward-looking, and strategic framework**.

Through ALM and consistent stress testing, banks can achieve a **holistic view of resilience**, balancing capital strength, liquidity adequacy, and earnings stability.

Such integration not only satisfies supervisory expectations but also enhances **strategic agility**, allowing institutions to anticipate shocks, optimize capital and funding resources, and maintain stakeholder confidence.

In an environment of renewed interest rate volatility, ESG transition pressures, and digital transformation, a **holistic balance sheet view** is no longer optional — it is the **core of sustainable banking management**.

# **Chapter 13 – Case Study 1: Interest Rate Risk and ALM Optimization under Monetary Tightening**

#### **13.1 Introduction**

The post-pandemic period marked the fastest global monetary tightening cycle in decades. Central banks across advanced economies—most notably the **Federal Reserve**, the **European Central Bank (ECB)**, and the **Bank of England**—raised policy rates by several hundred basis points between 2022 and 2023 in response to persistent inflationary pressures.

For banks, this new regime fundamentally reshaped **balance sheet dynamics**. Net interest margins initially expanded due to asset repricing, but **interest rate risk** and **mark-to-market volatility** increased sharply, especially for institutions holding long-duration sovereign or mortgage portfolios.

This case study examines how a representative mid-size **European commercial bank ("Bank A")** optimized its ALM positioning during the tightening phase of 2022–2023, balancing profitability, duration risk, and capital impacts under regulatory constraints (IRRBB and ICAAP/ILAAP integration).

# **Chapter 15 – Case Study 3: Managing Non-Maturing Deposits (NMDs) and Behavioral Optionality in a Low-Rate Environment**

# **Chapter 17 – Case Study 5: Liquidity Buffer Optimization and the LCR/NSFR Trade-Off**

## **17.1 Introduction**

Liquidity management is one of the foundational pillars of **Asset & Liability Management (ALM)**, ensuring that a bank can meet its short-term obligations while maintaining balance sheet efficiency.

Under the **Basel III framework**, liquidity is measured through two key regulatory indicators:

- the **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)**, which assesses short-term resilience to stressed funding scenarios, and
- the **Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)**, which measures structural funding stability over a one-year horizon.

# **Chapter 18 – Case Study 6: Hedging Fixed-Rate Assets under the IFRS 9 Macro Fair Value Hedge Framework**

# **18.1 Introduction**

Interest rate volatility represents one of the most significant sources of valuation and earnings risk for banks. In particular, **fixed-rate assets**—such as long-term mortgages, leasing contracts, and medium-to-long-term corporate loans—expose banks to **fair value risk** when market rates fluctuate.

Under the **IFRS 9 accounting framework**, banks can apply **Macro Fair Value Hedge (MFVH)** accounting to mitigate such risks, aligning the measurement of hedging derivatives and hedged items to avoid artificial volatility in the income statement.

This chapter explores the **design, implementation, and implications** of a macro fair value hedge strategy applied to a portfolio of fixed-rate loans. The case study focuses on "**Bank F**," a medium-sized European bank seeking to stabilize its **economic value of equity (EVE)** and its **net interest income (NII)** profile amid a scenario of declining interest rates.

## **18.2 Regulatory and Accounting Context**

Under **IFRS 9**, a **fair value hedge** aims to offset changes in the fair value of an exposure attributable to a specific risk—in this case, interest rate risk.

For banks, applying the **Macro Fair Value Hedge (MFVH)** model enables:

- Consistent accounting treatment between hedged items (e.g., fixed-rate assets) and hedging instruments (e.g., interest rate swaps);
- Reduction of **P&L volatility** arising from fair value changes of derivatives;
- Alignment with internal ALM risk metrics such as **ΔEVE** and **ΔNII**.

EBA and ESMA guidelines (EBA/GL/2020/07; ESMA Public Statement 2021/353) emphasize the need for consistent risk identification, documentation of the hedge relationship, and robust testing of hedge effectiveness—both **ex ante** (prospective) and **ex post** (retrospective).

# **Chapter 19 – Case Study 7: Behavioral Modelling of Non-Maturing Deposits (NMDs) and Interest Rate Sensitivity**

# **19.1 Introduction**

Among the most distinctive challenges in bank Asset & Liability Management (ALM) is the treatment of **Non-Maturing Deposits (NMDs)**—such as current accounts, savings accounts, and sight deposits—which lack contractual maturities yet exhibit **stable behavioral patterns**.

Unlike term deposits, NMDs do not have a defined repricing date. Their economic duration depends on depositor behavior, interest rate sensitivity, and competitive dynamics in retail funding markets.

Therefore, accurate modeling of NMDs is essential to correctly assess a bank's **interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB)** and ensure alignment with **regulatory (EBA/GL/2018/02)** and **accounting frameworks (IFRS 9)**.

This chapter presents a **quantitative and behavioral case study** on how a mid-sized retail bank—**Bank G**—models its NMD portfolio to estimate duration, elasticity to interest rates, and implications for both **ΔNII** and **ΔEVE** metrics.

## **19.2 Conceptual Framework**

NMDs represent a **hybrid liability**:

- From a contractual perspective, they are repayable on demand.
- From a behavioral perspective, a portion of them behaves as **"core deposits"**, providing stable, long-term funding to the bank.

To capture this duality, the ALM function typically estimates:

- 1. **Core vs. Volatile share** of deposits;
- 2. **Effective duration** (behavioral maturity) of the stable component;
- 3. **Repricing elasticity** to market rate changes (pass-through rate).

According to the EBA and Basel guidelines (BCBS 368, 2016), modeling must ensure:

- Stability and statistical significance of behavioral estimates;
- Periodic back-testing and validation;
- Consistency with liquidity metrics (LCR, NSFR).

# **19.4 Behavioral Modelling Methodology**

Bank G employs a **multi-factor econometric approach** integrating both **historical timeseries** and **panel regression analysis** to estimate behavioral parameters:

$$r_{dep,t} = \alpha + \beta_1 \cdot r_{mkt,t} + \beta_2 \cdot \Delta r_{mkt,t} + \beta_3 \cdot \pi_t + \varepsilon_t$$

# Where:

• , : average deposit rate at time *t*;

• , : market reference rate (e.g., 3M Euribor);

• : inflation rate;

• 1: long-run pass-through coefficient;

• 2: short-run rate adjustment sensitivity.

## **Empirical results (2012–2024 sample):**

• Long-term pass-through (1) ≈ 0.30 → only 30% of market rate changes are transferred to customers;

• Short-term adjustment (2) ≈ 0.12 → gradual repricing effect;

• Inflation correlation (3) ≈ 0.10 → partial sensitivity to price dynamics.

# **19.5 Stability and Core Deposit Estimation**

Using a **survival function** approach, the bank estimates the **average life (AL)** of deposits as:

$$AL = \int_0^\infty S(t) dt$$

where ()represents the probability that a deposit remains outstanding at time *t*. The model results indicate that roughly **63% of total NMDs** behave as stable core deposits with an **average behavioral maturity of 3 years**.

Bank G validates these results via:

- **Cohort tracking** (withdrawal probabilities over time);
- **Stress-testing** under rate shocks (±200 bps);
- **Benchmark comparison** with peer institutions and EBA calibration tables.

## **5. Final Message**

The journey of ALM—from duration matching in the 1980s, through VaR-based frameworks post-2008, to behavioral and AI-enhanced modelling today—reflects the evolution of banking itself.

In an environment characterized by rapid rate cycles, digital transformation, and regulatory tightening, **strategic ALM** becomes a source of **competitive advantage** rather than a mere compliance necessity.

The central message of this book is thus both simple and profound:

"Banks that treat ALM as an intelligence function—integrating risk, capital, liquidity, and behavior—will not only survive future rate shocks but will thrive on them."
